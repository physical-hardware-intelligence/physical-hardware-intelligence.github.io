The field

Everyone says they are scaling. Almost nobody says what.

Robotics has one word for eight different bets. Each of them buys you something real, charges you something real, and leaves you blind to something real. This is the tree. Open a trunk to see the branches, open a branch to see who is out on it.

01 Scale the data 8 branches

The default answer, and the only one most people mean. But data is not one thing — it is eight different bets, and each buys you something by blinding you to something else.

Let the robot try it itself
Buys you
Correct by construction
Costs you
Slowest, and it needs a score
Blind to
Anything you cannot write a reward for
  • Physical IntelligenceRECAP: demonstrations, then corrections, then self-improvement from autonomous trials
  • Dyna Roboticsone task pushed to commercial reliability before adding the next
Put a human on the controls
Buys you
Perfectly aligned with the robot's own body
Costs you
An hour of human time per hour of data
Blind to
Force — a remote operator cannot feel anything
  • XDOFruns the teleoperation collection that AI labs would rather not own
  • Sanctuary AIteleoperation first, on the argument that you cannot automate what you cannot yet do
  • Toyota Research Institutebimanual haptic teleop — the rare version where the operator does feel
Give the human the robot's hands, then remove the robot
Buys you
Data scales with people, not with arms
Costs you
The arm behind the hand is still a human arm
Blind to
Viewpoint and whole-body consistency
  • Sunday Roboticsa $200 glove matching Memo's hands exactly, in 500+ homes
  • Proceptiona sensor glove feeding a 22-degree-of-freedom hand
Borrow other robots' data
Buys you
Hundreds of thousands of episodes, already recorded
Costs you
You inherit their calibration and their cameras
Blind to
Your own gripper's quirks
  • Skild AIone omni-bodied policy across quadrupeds, humanoids and factory arms
Take it from humans on video
Buys you
Free, and effectively unlimited
Costs you
No actions, ever
Blind to
What to do — video only shows what happened
  • Asimovpays people to record their own routines on a head-mounted phone
  • Niantic Spatialthirty billion posed images turned into geometry
Manufacture it in a simulator
Buys you
Infinite, free and resettable
Costs you
Wrong in exactly the place contact happens
Blind to
Friction, deformation, compliance
  • Eka Roboticscontact fidelity over photorealism: mass, inertia, friction
  • Genesis AIa physics engine used to manufacture the training set
  • Antim Labsa prompt or a floorplan becomes a simulator-ready scene
  • Dirac Roboticsasset physics measured against real hardware instead of eyeballed
  • AuraMLa multimodal world simulator aimed at the sim-to-real gap
Multiply the real data you already have
Buys you
Cheapest possible gain per episode
Costs you
Cannot create what was never recorded
Blind to
Anything outside the span of your originals
  • MimicGen, DemoGen, RoVi-Augthe augmentation line on the Learn page's data spine
Let robots that already have a job collect it
Buys you
The only data that pays for itself
Costs you
Only exists for tasks narrow enough to deploy today
Blind to
Anything nobody will pay for yet
  • Ambi Roboticstwenty-two years of robot data, collected by sorting a hundred million real parcels
  • Addverb TechnologiesIndia's largest deployed fleet
  • Ati Roboticstwo million industrial missions a year
  • Burroa quarter of a million autonomous field miles
02 Scale the bodies 4 branches

A policy is worth more if it runs on more machines, and a machine is worth less if it costs more. Four different ways out, and two of them are about not building a robot at all.

One policy, many morphologies
Buys you
Every new body multiplies the training set
Costs you
Nothing is tuned for anything
Blind to
The last few percent, which is where deployment lives
  • Skild AIomni-bodied by design — humans counted as another form factor
  • Open X-Embodimentthe pooled dataset that made the argument possible
One body, mass-manufactured
Buys you
Unit cost falls with volume, like every other machine
Costs you
You must be right about the design before you tool up
Blind to
Anything the form factor cannot reach
  • Figure AIa factory whose output is measured in robots per hour
  • Apptronikforce-controlled actuators built around a manufacturing thesis
Retrofit machines that already exist
Buys you
Skips the hardest and slowest part entirely
Costs you
You inherit somebody else's mechanism
Blind to
Anything needing a body nobody has built
  • Bedrock Roboticsautonomy fitted to excavators and dozers already on site
  • Bonsai Roboticsa kit into existing orchard equipment rather than a new vehicle
Make the body cheap enough to stop mattering
Buys you
Volume, and a community that can afford to experiment
Costs you
Precision, stiffness and lifetime
Blind to
Anything needing real payload or repeatability
  • Cosine Robotsa $4,000 humanoid from automotive actuators
  • Menlo Researchan open-source humanoid you can print for around $15,000
  • SO-101the arm this club actually runs, and the reason any of this is legible to us

Buys you

Costs you
Rewards get worse before the method gets better
Blind to
Tasks nobody can describe as a score

Buys you

08 Scale the measuring 3 branches

The trunk with almost nothing growing on it. Seven axes above, and no trustworthy way to compare progress along any of them. This is the honest state of the field.

Benchmarks
Buys you
Comparability, cheaply
Costs you
They are not floors, and everyone knows it
Blind to
Real environments, which is the entire point
  • COLOSSEUM and VLATestthe benchmark branch on the Learn page's deployment spine
Predict real performance in simulation
Buys you
Evaluation without a physical reset
Costs you
The gap you are measuring is the gap you cannot model
Blind to
Contact, again
  • SIMPLERfiled under exactly this question on the Learn page
Measure real robots honestly, with statistics
Buys you
Numbers that survive contact with a reviewer
Costs you
Every rollout is a physical reset, so n stays tiny
Blind to
Nothing. This is the correct answer and almost nobody pays for it
  • Toyota Research InstituteA Careful Examination of Large Behavior Models — where the gains got smaller under scrutiny
  • Sunday Roboticsa 50-household beta makes this strictly harder: you cannot reset a real kitchen

Why the last trunk is nearly bare

Seven axes with real money on them, and one with almost nothing: nobody is scaling the ability to tell whether any of it worked. Every rollout on a real robot costs a physical reset, so sample sizes stay small and the error bars stay wide. When one group did measure carefully, the reported gains got smaller.

That is not a swipe at anyone. It is the most useful thing on this page, and it is why a four-person club with one arm can still say something true: the cheap experiment is a better measurement, not a bigger model.
