Study guide · arXiv 2610.12465 · CoRL 2026

Deriving Success Guided Sampling

A walkthrough of A Balanced Data Diet: Addressing the Exploration Bottleneck in Mega-Scale RL for Robot Control by Octi Zhang, Mateo Guaman Castro, Patrick Yin, Ignacio Dagnino, Abhishek Gupta, Rosario Scalise and Byron Boots (University of Washington; Zhang is also at NVIDIA). Every equation is derived. Every number was checked against the paper's LaTeX source and its figure files.

Problem
Massively parallel simulation plus diverse resets makes hard robot tasks reachable. Uniform sampling then spends a growing fraction of episodes on configurations the policy always solves or never solves, and those episodes carry almost no gradient.
Fix
Keep a rolling success rate for every configuration. Sample configurations near a target success rate, using a Beta-shaped weight and a softmax. That is the whole method.
Result
At about one million environments: 73% success on 13-terrain quadruped locomotion (PLR 54%) and 70% on Franka nut-and-bolt assembly (uniform 6%, PLR 5%). RGB students distilled from SGS teachers transfer zero-shot to a UR5e on three NIST assembly tasks.

1Why this paper exists

RL in massively parallel simulators (MuJoCo, Isaac Gym and Isaac Lab, ManiSkill) now produces controllers that work on real robots. The price is per-task engineering. Rewards are shaped and tuned by hand, curricula are ordered by hand, demonstrations are collected to start exploration, and multi-skill controllers are often built by training specialists and distilling them into one policy. Each choice brings new hyperparameters.

Resets are a cheaper lever. If some episodes start with the rod already half inside the hole, the policy can reach success early, learn what success looks like, and then extend the skill back to harder starts. OmniReset (P. Yin et al., 2026, arXiv 2603.15789) generates diverse resets programmatically and pairs them with massively parallel RL. This paper builds on it; P. Yin and I. Dagnino are authors of both.

Resets decide where an episode can start. They do not decide how often each start gets practised. That allocation problem is the subject of the paper.

Intuition

A coach has a million students and one rule: every student practises a random drill. Most drills are already mastered or still impossible for this team, so most practice is wasted. Hiring more students buys more of the same mix. SGS changes the rule: practise the drills you currently pass about half the time.

Paper

The authors say plainly that SGS is not a new curriculum principle. Their claim is empirical: a simple success-based sampler, combined with diverse resets and very large PPO runs, solves locomotion and assembly tasks that uniform sampling and PLR learn poorly or not at all, and beats a hand-designed curriculum and SFL where those were tested.

2Prerequisites

Seven ideas the paper uses without defining them. Each is built here from scratch, once.

2.1The goal-conditioned MDP

A Markov decision process (MDP) is the formal game RL plays. Goal-conditioned means the policy is also told what to achieve, so one policy can serve many goals.

\[\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S},\ \mathcal{A},\ \mathcal{G},\ P,\ r,\ \gamma,\ \rho)\]
SymbolNameLocomotion (ANYmal-D)Manipulation (UR5e, Franka)
\(s\in\mathcal{S}\)statefull physical state: base pose and velocity, joint angles and velocities, terrainarm joints, gripper, object and board poses
\(a\in\mathcal{A}\)action12 joint-position offsets from the nominal pose3 translation and 3 rotation increments plus open/close (UR5e; see §8)
\(g\in\mathcal{G}\)goala goal pose on the terrainthe assembled pose relative to the receptacle
\(P(s'\mid s,a)\)dynamicsthe physics simulator
\(r(s,a,g)\)rewardone shared reward per domain (§4)
\(\gamma\)discounta number below 1; the paper does not report its value
\(\rho\)start-and-goal distributioncategorical over 104,000 configurationscategorical over 32,768 configurations

One episode: draw \((s_0,g)\sim\rho\). At every step \(t\) the policy picks \(a_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_t,g)\), physics produces \(s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t)\), and the robot receives \(r(s_t,a_t,g)\). The objective is the expected discounted return:

\[J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{(s_0,g)\sim\rho,\ a_t\sim\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,r(s_t,a_t,g)\Big]\]

Read it from the inside out. Inside the brackets: the rewards of one episode, each multiplied by \(\gamma^t\) so later rewards count less. Outside: the average over many episodes, where the randomness comes from which start and goal \(\rho\) draws and from the policy's random actions.

Paper

RL is trained on "compact Lagrangian states". Lagrangian state means generalized coordinates and their velocities, \((q,\dot q)\): the few numbers that fully describe a mechanism's configuration and motion, as opposed to pixels. Images enter only later, in the distilled student (§7). The observation lists in §4 also include a terrain height scan and a point cloud.

2.2Task configurations

The paper's unit of choice is a task configuration:

\[\tau=(s_0,\ g,\ e)\qquad\text{start state, goal, environment}\]
LocomotionManipulation
\(s_0\)the robot's start pose on the terraininitial robot and object state, from one of three reset families (§5.1)
\(g\)a goal pose to reachthe assembled pose, identical for every configuration in the receptacle's frame
\(e\)terrain type and geometrywhich assembly task, plus the receptacle pose

Before training, \(N\) configurations are drawn once and frozen. SGS only ever chooses among these \(N\).

Check

§3.1 defines \(\rho\) over \((s_0,g)\) and says \(s_0\) already contains all episode-level variation, terrain and objects included. The extra \(e\) in \(\tau\) is a label for that variation, not a new ingredient.

2.3Policy gradients, GAE and PPO

All RL training in the paper uses PPO; the RGB students are distilled with DAgger (§7.3). Four formulas carry the whole algorithm.

Derivation

Advantage: how much better action \(a\) is than what the policy usually does from \(s\).

\[A^{\pi}(s,a)=Q^{\pi}(s,a)-V^{\pi}(s)\]

Policy gradient: raise the probability of better-than-usual actions, lower the probability of worse-than-usual ones.

\[\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t,g)\;A_t\Big]\]

GAE (generalized advantage estimation) estimates \(A_t\) from a learned critic \(V\). The TD error \(\delta_t\) is one step of surprise; GAE adds up future surprises with weight \((\gamma\lambda)^l\).

\[\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t),\qquad \hat A_t=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}\,\delta_{t+l}\]

\(\lambda=0\) trusts the critic (one step, low variance, biased if \(V\) is wrong). \(\lambda=1\) trusts the actual return (unbiased, high variance).

PPO takes that gradient step but stops pushing an action's probability once it has moved more than \(\pm\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\) relative to the policy that collected the data:

\[r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t,g)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t,g)},\qquad L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon_{\text{clip}},1+\epsilon_{\text{clip}})\,\hat A_t\big)\Big]\]

Batch bookkeeping. One PPO iteration collects \(E\) environments \(\times\ n\) steps, splits the batch into \(M\) minibatches, and runs \(K\) epochs over them: \(M\times K\) gradient steps per iteration.

Paper

The minibatch count \(M\) is held fixed while \(E\) grows, and every other PPO and optimizer setting is fixed too. So more environments make each minibatch bigger (a less noisy gradient), not the number of gradient steps larger. The paper does not report \(\gamma\), \(\lambda\), \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\), \(K\), \(n\), \(M\) or the learning rate.

2.4Why a 0% or 100% configuration teaches nothing

This is the paper's whole argument in one derivation.

Derivation

Fix one configuration. Idealize the reward the way both domains are built (§4): the return is \(R_s\) on success and \(R_f\) otherwise, and the critic knows this configuration's success probability \(p\), so \(V(s_0)=pR_s+(1-p)R_f\). Let \(\Delta R=R_s-R_f\). The advantage of a whole episode is the return minus the baseline:

\[A=R-V(s_0)=\begin{cases}(1-p)\,\Delta R & \text{success, probability } p\\[2pt] -p\,\Delta R & \text{no success, probability } 1-p\end{cases}\]

Its mean, mean square and mean absolute value:

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[A] &= p(1-p)\Delta R-(1-p)p\,\Delta R=0\\ \mathbb{E}[A^2] &= p(1-p)^2\Delta R^2+(1-p)p^2\Delta R^2=p(1-p)\,\Delta R^2\,\big[(1-p)+p\big]=p(1-p)\,\Delta R^2\\ \mathbb{E}|A| &= p\,(1-p)\Delta R+(1-p)\,p\,\Delta R=2p(1-p)\,\Delta R \end{aligned}\]
  • At \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) the advantage is zero on every episode, so that configuration adds exactly zero task gradient.
  • The signal is largest at \(p=\tfrac12\), where \(\mathbb{E}|A|=\Delta R/2\).
  • \(p(1-p)\) is the variance of a coin that lands heads with probability \(p\). A configuration is useful exactly when its outcome is uncertain.
MethodScore for a configurationRelation to the derivation
SFL\(p(1-p)\)exactly \(\mathbb{E}[A^2]/\Delta R^2\)
PLRaverage \(|\hat A_t|\) over the last episodea critic-based estimate of the same kind of quantity; it inherits the critic's errors
SGS\(p^{\kappa t^\star}(1-p)^{\kappa(1-t^\star)}\)generalizes \(p(1-p)\): movable peak \(t^\star\), adjustable sharpness \(\kappa\). Equal to \(p(1-p)\) at \(t^\star=0.5,\ \kappa=2\)

Check

Two small leaks remain at \(p\in\{0,1\}\). With \(\gamma\lt 1\), succeeding sooner earns more discounted return, and the per-step regularizers always vary a little. Neither one teaches the task itself, which is why the paper treats such configurations as wasted.

2.5The Beta distribution, as a weighting curve

The Beta distribution lives on \([0,1]\), the same range as a success rate:

\[f(x;\alpha,\beta)=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)},\qquad x\in[0,1],\quad \alpha,\beta>0\]

\(B(\alpha,\beta)\) only makes the area equal 1. SGS never needs it, because the softmax in §2.6 normalizes anyway.

Derivation: where is the peak?

Take the log and set the derivative to zero:

\[\frac{d}{dx}\log f=\frac{\alpha-1}{x}-\frac{\beta-1}{1-x}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;(\alpha-1)(1-x)=(\beta-1)\,x\;\Longrightarrow\;x^\star=\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+\beta-2}\]

This is a maximum when \(\alpha,\beta>1\). Now use the paper's mode–concentration form:

\[\alpha=1+\kappa t^\star,\quad\beta=1+\kappa(1-t^\star)\;\Longrightarrow\;x^\star=\frac{\kappa t^\star}{\kappa}=t^\star,\qquad f\propto x^{\kappa t^\star}(1-x)^{\kappa(1-t^\star)}\]

So \(t^\star\) is exactly the peak, and \(\kappa=\alpha+\beta-2\) sets how sharp it is.

\(\kappa\)\(t^\star\)\(\alpha,\ \beta\)Shape
0any1, 1flat: every success rate weighted equally, which is uniform sampling
20.52, 2\(x(1-x)\): SFL's learnability score
large\(t^\star\)\(\approx\kappa t^\star,\ \kappa(1-t^\star)\)a narrow spike at \(t^\star\)

Width, for \(t^\star=0.5\), from the Beta variance \(\alpha\beta/[(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)]\) with \(\alpha=\beta=1+\kappa/2\):

\[\operatorname{Var}=\frac{1}{4(\kappa+3)},\qquad \text{sd}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\kappa+3}}\]

\(\kappa=0\) gives \(1/\sqrt{12}\approx0.289\), the standard deviation of a uniform variable on \([0,1]\), as it should. \(\kappa=1\) gives 0.250, \(\kappa=5\) gives 0.177, \(\kappa=10\) gives 0.139.

Intuition

Nothing in SGS is ever drawn from a Beta distribution. The Beta curve is used only as a scoring function: feed in a success rate, get back a weight. Statistics texts (Kruschke's form) call \(\alpha+\beta\) the concentration; the paper's \(\kappa\) is that number minus 2.

2.6Softmax with a temperature

A softmax turns scores \(\ell_i\) (logits) into probabilities. The temperature \(T\) flattens it:

\[q_i=\frac{\exp(\ell_i/T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\ell_j/T)}\]

Derivation: what the temperature does here

SGS uses logits \(\ell_i=\log(\tilde w_i+\epsilon)\), where \(\tilde w_i\) is the floored kernel weight (§3.2). Exponentiating undoes the log:

\[\exp(\ell_i/T)=(\tilde w_i+\epsilon)^{1/T}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_i=\frac{(\tilde w_i+\epsilon)^{1/T}}{\sum_j(\tilde w_j+\epsilon)^{1/T}}\]

Wherever the weight is far above the floor (\(w_i\gg\epsilon\)):

\[q_i\;\propto\;w_i^{1/T}=(p_i+\epsilon)^{\frac{\kappa}{T}t^\star}\,(1-p_i+\epsilon)^{\frac{\kappa}{T}(1-t^\star)}\]

That is the same Beta shape with the same peak \(t^\star\) and concentration \(\kappa/T\).

  • In the interior, \(\kappa\) and \(T\) are one knob. Only \(\kappa/T\) matters. This is why the authors sweep \(\kappa\) and leave \(T\) at 2; the paper says \(T\) is "tied to" \(\kappa\).
  • At the floor, \(T\) matters separately. Floored configurations get \((2\epsilon)^{1/T}\), so the peak-to-floor ratio is \(\big((w_{\text{peak}}+\epsilon)/2\epsilon\big)^{1/T}\). A larger \(T\) flattens the curve and lifts the floor.
  • The implementation uses \(T_{\text{eff}}=\max(T,1)\). The softmax can only flatten the raw kernel, never sharpen it.

2.7Estimating a success rate from \(H\) outcomes

Each configuration keeps its last \(H\) outcomes \(o_{i,1},\dots,o_{i,H}\in\{0,1\}\) and estimates

\[p_i=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{k=1}^{H}o_{i,k}\in\Big\{0,\ \tfrac1H,\ \tfrac2H,\ \dots,\ 1\Big\}\]

Derivation: how noisy is it?

If the true success probability \(\pi_i\) stayed fixed, \(H\,p_i\) would be Binomial\((H,\pi_i)\), so

\[\mathbb{E}[p_i]=\pi_i,\qquad \operatorname{SE}(p_i)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi_i(1-\pi_i)}{H}}\;\le\;\frac{1}{2\sqrt H}\]

Worst-case standard error: 0.10 at \(H=25\), 0.05 at \(H=100\), 0.025 at \(H=400\).

The policy keeps changing, so the window mixes old and new behavior. A long window means less noise but more lag. That trade-off matches the paper's sweep: \(H=25\) scores 0.39, \(H=100\) scores 0.49, \(H=400\) scores 0.47 (two seeds each, §6.1).

Check: scale makes the estimates fresher

Under uniform sampling, one round of episodes (every environment finishes one) gives each configuration \(E/N\) attempts on average. For locomotion (\(N=104{,}000\)) that is 0.04 at 4,096 environments and 10 at \(2^{20}\). Filling a 100-outcome window therefore takes about 2,500 rounds at 4K and about 10 rounds at 1M, so estimates are roughly 256 times fresher at the largest scale. For manipulation (\(N=32{,}768\)) the figures are 0.125 and 32 attempts per round. The paper does not make this point; it follows from the arithmetic. Under SGS itself, configurations near the peak are visited more often (fresher) and floored ones less often (staler).

3SGS, line by line

3.1The loop

before training
  draw N configurations τ_1 … τ_N from the task distribution     (frozen for the run)
  give each configuration a ring buffer of its last H outcomes

whenever an environment finishes an episode on configuration i
  push 1 (success) or 0 (no success) into buffer i
  p_i ← mean of buffer i
  w_i ← (p_i + ε)^(κ·t*) · (1 − p_i + ε)^(κ·(1 − t*))
  ℓ_i ← log( max(w_i, ε) + ε )
  this environment's next configuration:  j ~ softmax( ℓ / max(T, 1) )

What SGS does not use: no value function, no learned model, no extra evaluation rollouts, no difficulty ordering. Its only signal is the success bit each training episode already produces.

Check

The pseudocode shows the conceptual order. The paper does not say how often scores are recomputed in its batched GPU implementation, or what an empty buffer's mean is. Fig. 7 shows that sampling starts out uniform, which is what you get when every \(p_i\) starts equal: if all are 0, every configuration gets the same weight.

3.2Equation 1: the kernel

The paper's Eq. 1, with the target written \(t^\star\):

\[w_i=(p_i+\epsilon)^{\kappa t^\star}\,(1-p_i+\epsilon)^{\kappa(1-t^\star)},\qquad \ell_i=\log(w_i+\epsilon)\]
PieceJob
\((p_i+\epsilon)^{\kappa t^\star}\)grows with success. Near zero when \(p_i=0\), which pushes impossible configurations down.
\((1-p_i+\epsilon)^{\kappa(1-t^\star)}\)grows with failure. Near zero when \(p_i=1\), which pushes mastered configurations down.
the productpeaks at \(p_i=t^\star\) (§2.5)

The implementation (Appendix B.2) adds a floor before the log and a minimum temperature:

\[\tilde w_i=\max(w_i,\epsilon),\qquad \ell_i=\log(\tilde w_i+\epsilon),\qquad T_{\text{eff}}=\max(T,1)\]

The floor guarantees every configuration a minimum weight. That keeps impossible and mastered configurations in rotation, so their estimates get refreshed when the policy changes.

Check: when does the floor actually bind?

Locomotion (\(\epsilon=10^{-8}\), \(\kappa=5\), \(t^\star=0.66\)): \(w(0)\approx4\times10^{-27}\) and \(w(1)\approx2.5\times10^{-14}\), both floored. One success in 100 already gives \(w(0.01)\approx2.5\times10^{-7}\), above the floor. So only exactly-0% and exactly-100% configurations sit on it. Manipulation (\(\epsilon=10^{-4}\), \(\kappa=1\), \(t^\star=0.5\)): the smallest weight is \(w(0)=w(1)\approx0.01\), a hundred times the floor, so the floor never binds.

3.3Equation 2: from weights to a draw

At the end of every episode, that environment's next configuration is drawn independently from

\[q_i=\frac{\exp(\ell_i/T_{\text{eff}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\ell_j/T_{\text{eff}})}\;=\;\frac{(\tilde w_i+\epsilon)^{1/T_{\text{eff}}}}{\sum_{j}(\tilde w_j+\epsilon)^{1/T_{\text{eff}}}}\]

With \(T=2\) this is a square root of the floored weight, renormalized. A configuration's probability depends on its own success rate and, through the denominator, on everyone else's.

Check

The paper says \(r_{\text{fwd}}\) makes the robot walk forward instead of approaching the goal backwards. As written, it is a dot product of two unit vectors in the same frame, and a dot product does not depend on the frame. So the formula alone measures "moving toward the goal"; to reward facing forward, one of the two vectors must be tied to the body's heading. The paper does not define \(\hat v\) and \(\hat g\) further, and does not say whether the 500 steps are environment steps or PPO iterations.

\(r_{\dot q}\)\(-\lambda_{\dot q}\,C(\lVert\dot q_{t,\text{arm}}\rVert_2^2)\)\(10^{-4}\)arm joint speed

Check: what the clamp implies

Each squared norm is capped at \(10^4\), so the per-step regularizers can never cost more than \(10^{-5}\cdot10^{4}+10^{-4}\cdot10^{4}+10^{-4}\cdot10^{4}=0.1+1+1=2.1\) per step. Failure costs 1 and success pays 100, so a failed attempt is nearly free; the failure term works mainly as a safety stop.

Intuition

Both domains put nearly all of the reward into one boolean outcome. That is what makes "success rate" a meaningful single number for SGS to steer by.

5Experiments

Three questions: does SGS scale better than uniform sampling and PLR as environments grow (Q1), does it unlock tasks the others cannot learn even at small scale (Q2), and do its manipulation policies transfer to real hardware (Q3)?

5.1Tasks and configuration spaces

Locomotion. ANYmal-D with system-identified actuator models. Thirteen terrain types: slopes, pits, gaps and stairs from earlier work; radiating beams, balance beams and stepping stones; harder procedurally generated terrains (navigation mazes, contoured terrain, floating islands joined by narrow stepping stones or jump boxes at different heights); and an inverted slope, which Fig. 3 omits for space. 5.2The baselines

SamplerHow it picks the next configurationUsed on
Uniforma fresh draw from \(\rho\) at every reset. Enough for easier manipulation in OmniReset; not effective for multi-task locomotion.all

Derivation: PLR in full (Jiang, Grefenstette and Rocktäschel, ICML 2021)

Score of level \(i\) from its most recent episode of length \(T_{\text{ep}}\):

\[S_i=\frac{1}{T_{\text{ep}}}\sum_{t=0}^{T_{\text{ep}}}\Big|\sum_{k=t}^{T_{\text{ep}}}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\,\delta_k\Big|\]

Rank-based prioritization with temperature \(\beta\), mixed with staleness (\(c\) = episodes so far, \(C_i\) = episode count when level \(i\) was last played):

\[P_S(i)=\frac{h(S_i)^{1/\beta}}{\sum_j h(S_j)^{1/\beta}},\quad h(S_i)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{rank}(S_i)},\qquad P_C(i)=\frac{c-C_i}{\sum_j (c-C_j)},\qquad P(i)=(1-\rho_{\text{st}})P_S(i)+\rho_{\text{st}}P_C(i)\]

Formulas from the PLR paper; I write its staleness weight \(\rho\) as \(\rho_{\text{st}}\) to avoid a clash. The SGS paper does not report the \(\beta\) and \(\rho_{\text{st}}\) it used.

Derivation: SFL in full (Rutherford et al., NeurIPS 2024)

Every \(T\) updates: roll out \(N\) random levels for \(L\) steps, compute each one's success rate \(p\) and learnability \(p(1-p)\), keep the top \(K\). Each update then trains on \(\rho N_L\) levels drawn uniformly from the kept set plus \((1-\rho)N_L\) fresh random levels. Published defaults: \(T=50\), \(\rho=0.5\), \(N_L=256\), \(L=2000\), \(N=5000\), \(K=1000\).

From the SFL paper. The SGS paper says it ran SFL with default hyperparameters.

7Sim-to-real

Pipeline: train a point-cloud teacher with SGS in simulation, distill it into an RGB student with DAgger, run the student on a real UR5e with no real-world data or fine-tuning. It builds on OmniReset's transfer pipeline.

7.1Hardware and the board

  • UR5e with a Robotiq 2F-85, OmniReset's relative Cartesian operational-space control, one third-person camera and one wrist camera (OmniReset used three cameras).
  • The NIST board is polished acrylic. Facing it, the wrist camera saw the robot's own reflection, an image it never saw in training. The board's low friction also broke strategies that push objects against it to reorient them. The fix was covering the board with tape.
  • The board is fixed to the table with command strips. Simulation treats the receptacles as static, so a board that shifts on contact causes failures.

7.2Gripper pad friction: the "glue grasp" trap

Derivation: why μ = 100 is a cheat

Coulomb friction caps the sideways force a contact can resist at \(\mu\) times the squeezing force:

\[|F_{\text{tangential}}|\le\mu\,F_{\text{normal}}\]

Squeeze with 10 N and \(\mu=100\) resists up to 1,000 N, so nothing ever slips: any touch with some normal force acts like glue. With \(\mu=2\), the same squeeze resists 20 N, and a badly placed grasp can fail as it would on the real robot.

  • OmniReset gave the pads \(\mu=100\) with the max combine mode. When two surfaces touch, a simulator needs one coefficient for the pair; max takes the larger of the two. Training was easy, and the authors found this a major source of sim-to-real gap.
  • Lowering friction removed the fake grasps but stopped learning altogether. A rod-in-hole sweep from scratch at \(\mu\in\{0.5,1,1.5,2\}\) (Fig. 9, measuring grasp-in-air success) trained stably only at \(\mu=2\). Every run uses \(\mu=2\).

7.3Teacher, student, and DAgger

Sees
Teacher actorarm joint positions, previous action, scene point cloud in the gripper frame
Teacher criticeverything the actor sees plus privileged state: joint velocities and the sampled randomization parameters
RGB studentside and wrist RGB images, each encoded by its own ResNet-18, plus arm joint positions, end-effector pose and previous action

The critic only exists during training, so it may see information the deployed robot never will. That is called an asymmetric actor–critic.

Why a point-cloud teacher. A teacher that reads exact object poses can act on information no camera provides. Point clouds are geometry, closer to what a student can infer from images, so the teacher's behavior is easier to imitate.

Derivation: DAgger versus behavioral cloning

Behavioral cloning trains on states the teacher visits. The student's small mistakes take it to states the teacher never visited, where it was never trained, and errors compound. DAgger (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011) lets the student act, asks the teacher what it would have done in each state the student reached, and trains on those labels. The training states are then the student's own.

The paper's online version: each iteration takes one step in every environment, updates the student toward the teacher's actions, and throws the data away. No dataset is stored, which saves GPU memory while simulation, rendering and training share the card.

  • Why hold out environments. In online DAgger, the student was just updated on the teacher's label for almost the same state one step earlier, so training success is inflated. The paper reserves 6.67% of environments (one in 15) that never contribute gradients. On the nut, held-out success lags training success early and catches up later (Fig. 10).
  • Distillation takes about two days on four L40S GPUs with 480 environments each: 1,920 environments, so the held-out share is 128 (32 per GPU), assuming the split is per GPU or global.
  • Visual randomization and image augmentation follow OmniReset.

7.4Symmetry-aware resets and rewards

Resets randomize each object over its equivalent orientations: discrete symmetries are sampled uniformly, and continuous ones uniformly over \([0^\circ,360^\circ)\). The success test accepts any equivalent goal orientation.

Intuition

If two orientations look identical to a camera but the teacher's reward insists on one of them, the teacher learns to tell them apart and the student cannot copy that. Symmetric resets and rewards remove distinctions the student cannot see.

8Reading it critically

The paper's three limitations

  1. Success estimates live on a discrete set of configurations. Continuous or much larger task spaces would need a learned or hierarchical score.
  2. SGS samples from a distribution; it does not build one. Coverage comes from reset generators like OmniReset, and choosing which configurations to offer is open.
  3. A sim-to-real gap remains.

Inconsistencies I found

How much the numbers can carry

  • Three seeds for the main results, two for the sensitivity table, one for the gravity ablation, and no intervals at all for Table 6.
  • Real-robot results rest on about 50 trials each, so their 95% intervals span 14 to 26 points (§7.5).
  • SFL is also reported at its best checkpoint, and it ran with its own paper's defaults while SGS had a small grid search.

Not in the paper, so not reproducible from it alone

PPO settings (\(\gamma\), \(\lambda\), \(\epsilon_{\text{clip}}\), epochs, rollout length, minibatch count, learning rate), episode lengths, PLR's \(\beta\) and staleness weight, how an empty buffer is scored, the Normal kernel's form, the gravity step size, the locomotion success tolerance, \(F_{\min}\), and the simulator.
