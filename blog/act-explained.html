ACT, explained from the ground up

One camera observation becomes a hundred coordinated actions. The architecture that made ten minutes of demonstrations enough for fine manipulation, taken apart.

Two robot arms hold a remote control between them. One keeps it still against a spring; the other slots a battery, catches the edge with a fingertip, and pushes it home. The entire policy learned from roughly ten minutes of a person doing the task.

The model behind it was not a vision-language model and it was not trained on the internet. It knew no nouns. It saw four camera frames, read fourteen joint positions, and predicted where fourteen joints should go next. Its important idea was about the word next: not the next action, but the next hundred.

That model is Action Chunking with Transformers, or ACT. It arrived with ALOHA in 2023, fit in roughly 80 million parameters, and became the baseline almost every low-cost robot lab trains first. Diffusion Policy, π0, SmolVLA and many of the policies that followed changed how an action sequence is generated. They kept the sequence.

Here is the original bimanual version. The chunk length k is configurable; the canonical experiments use roughly 90–100 steps. We draw k = 100 because it makes the central idea visible.

01The horizon is the problem

A standard behaviour-cloning policy is asked the same question at every control step: given what you see now, what should the robot do one fiftieth of a second from now? That sounds maximally reactive. It also turns a ten-second demonstration into five hundred separate chances to drift away from the data.

The policy was trained only on states a human visited. One slightly wrong target produces a slightly unfamiliar next state. The next prediction is made farther from the demonstrations, so it is easier to be wrong again. The errors do not add; they alter the input that creates the next error. This is .

ACT changes the unit of prediction. It asks for a short behaviour—reach toward the battery, close around it, move above the slot—rather than a single motor target. With a chunk of one hundred, a 400-step episode has an effective horizon of four chunks instead of four hundred isolated decisions. The transformer can model the correlation inside the motion instead of having to rediscover it frame by frame.

02A demonstration has a hidden variable

Chunking creates a second problem. From the same scene, one demonstrator might reach around the left side of an object and another around the right. A plain regressor trained with squared error is rewarded for averaging those futures. In robotics, the average of two valid paths is often a collision.

ACT therefore trains as a . A training-only encoder receives the current joints and the demonstrated action chunk. It compresses their particular manner—fast or slow, left or right, pause or continue—into a 32-number latent variable called z. The policy receives that style along with the observation and reconstructs the full chunk.

The loss pulls in two directions. L1 asks the predicted joint targets to match the demonstration. KL divergence pushes the distribution of styles toward a unit Gaussian, so the policy cannot use an unbounded private code for every trajectory.

Then comes the revealing detail. At deployment the style encoder is thrown away and the released implementation sets z to exactly zero, the mean of the prior. ACT is trained as a generative model but deployed as a deterministic one. The latent teaches the policy to organize the variation in human data; the robot does not sample a fresh personality on every rollout.

03DETR, with actions where objects used to be

The name says transformer, but vision is still a convolutional network. Each of the four camera frames goes through the same ResNet-18. Sharing matters: the wrist and room cameras do not each get a private backbone. Their feature maps are projected to the transformer's width and concatenated side by side, with two additional tokens carrying the current joints and z.

The rest is adapted from DETR, a model built for object detection. DETR has learned object queries: empty slots that ask an encoded image, in effect, which object belongs here? ACT reinterprets one hundred queries as one hundred future moments. Query 0 asks for the joint targets now; query 99 asks for the targets almost two seconds later.

Four transformer-encoder layers let image patches, joints, and style exchange information. Seven decoder layers let the action queries attend back to that memory. A final linear layer turns each 512-number decoder state into fourteen joint targets. All hundred queries run in parallel, so predicting a longer chunk is not the same as autoregressively waiting for a hundred model calls.

04The targets come from the leader, not the robot

ALOHA records two versions of every joint motion. The human moves lightweight leader arms. Heavier follower arms mirror them through a low-level controller. It is tempting to train on where the followers actually went, because those positions look like ground truth. ACT trains on the leader positions instead.

The difference encodes something the cameras do not: effort. If the leader keeps moving while a follower finger is blocked by a cup lid, the controller sees a larger position error and applies more force. Predicting the follower's stalled position would teach the policy to stop pushing at the exact moment pushing matters. Predicting the leader target preserves that implicit command.

This does not make ACT a force policy. It still emits target joint positions, and the tuned controller underneath decides what torques reach the motors. But it explains why the action label is not just a copy of the robot state one timestep later. The behaviour belongs partly to the learned model and partly to the controller tracking it.

05One hundred plans overlap at the present

If ACT executed an entire chunk before looking again, it would solve compounding error by giving up reactivity. The paper's second idea is to run the policy every timestep anyway. Each observation produces another hundred-step chunk. At time t, many earlier chunks contain a prediction for that same instant, made from different observations and at different look-ahead distances.

takes their weighted average. In the released code the coefficient is m = 0.01 and wi = exp(−m i), where i = 0 is the oldest prediction. That ordering is counterintuitive: older plans receive slightly more weight. They are farther from the current observation, but they are also less likely to twitch with the latest modeling noise. The result is continuity without waiting for a chunk boundary.

Temporal ensembling adds only 3.3 percentage points in the paper's aggregate ACT ablation, from 44% to 47.3%, but its visible effect is smoothness. Action chunking supplies a coherent plan; the ensemble keeps successive plans from disagreeing sharply at the motors.

06What the experiments actually establish

Each physical task used about fifty human demonstrations, typically eight to fourteen seconds each. The evaluation was not a language-general robot benchmark. It was the harder and more focused claim that one task-specific policy, trained from scratch on minutes of demonstrations, could control inexpensive and imprecise hardware through long, contact-rich sequences.

The ablations separate the ingredients unusually well. Increasing k from 1 to 100 changes aggregate success from 1% to 44%. Adding temporal ensembling raises the best ACT result to 47.3%. Removing the CVAE barely affects deterministic scripted demonstrations, but on human demonstrations success collapses from 35.3% to 2%.

So the transformer is not the main result despite being in the name. The main result is a representation of time. A single-step policy treats a pause as one hundred unrelated copies of “do nothing” and a grasp as a chain of fragile local guesses. A chunk represents each as one structured object. The CVAE explains why demonstrations of that object differ; temporal ensembling decides how consecutive objects overlap.

07What ACT leaves outside the model

ACT has no task language, no memory beyond the present observation, no force or tactile input, and no pretrained visual concepts. It trains a separate policy for each task. A four-camera ResNet can learn the battery and the remote from fifty demonstrations, but the black tail of a cable tie occupying a few pixels is enough to expose the limit.

Its two-second output is also not two seconds of understanding. Every number is a target position passed to a controller. Contact, stiffness, and torque remain implicit in the gap between leader and follower motion and in the gains of the hardware beneath the network. When the object moves unexpectedly, temporal ensembling lets a new visual observation bend the plan, but it does not give the policy a fast force-feedback reflex.

Those limits are why later policies changed the visual backbone, the generative objective, the data scale, or the action space. They are also why ACT remains useful. At our scale—one inexpensive arm, one camera setup, dozens rather than millions of episodes—it is the smallest serious answer to the whole problem: images and joints in, a smooth piece of behaviour out.

ACT is the baseline we recommend training before a VLA because its failure modes are legible. If it cannot solve a task, the answer is usually visible in the demonstrations, the camera view, the chunk, or the controller.
