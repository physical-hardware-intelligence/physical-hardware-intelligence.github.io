Deriving Squint

A walkthrough of Squint: Fast Visual Reinforcement Learning for Sim-to-Real Robotics by Abdulaziz Almuzairee and Henrik I. Christensen (UC San Diego), built up from Soft Actor-Critic. Every equation is derived or traced to its source. Every number was checked against the paper's LaTeX source, the released code (aalmuzairee/squint at commit 7086fd5), or the original SAC, TD3, C51, LayerNorm and time-limit papers. Numbers marked computed come from running that code; the script is published beside this page, with its output.

Problem
Off-policy RL reuses old experience but is slow in wall-clock time. On-policy PPO runs across thousands of simulators, but trains on each batch and then throws it away. Images make every part harder: high-dimensional input images complicate training dynamics, storing them in replay buffers strains memory, and encoding them through convolutional networks adds computational overhead (§I).
Fix
Keep SAC and make each part cheap: 1,024 parallel simulations with 256 updates per step, a distributional (C51) critic, images rendered at 128×128 and averaged down to 16×16 (squinting), LayerNorm after every hidden layer, a 1-million-transition replay buffer on the GPU, and compiled, CUDA-graph, bfloat16 updates.
Result
96.1% mean success over 8 SO-101 tasks in simulation after 15 minutes on one RTX 3090. Zero-shot on the real arm: 73 of 80 trials (91.3%); tuned SAC 65, DAgger 53, PPO 50, BC 38, DrQ-v2 8.

1Why this paper exists

Two clocks measure an RL method: environment steps (sample efficiency) and minutes (wall-clock). On a GPU simulator steps are cheap, so the minutes are what slow research down.

Paper, §I: the headline claim

To the best of our knowledge, Squint is the first method to demonstrate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer of a contact-rich stacking policy, trained entirely from scratch with no demonstrations, in under 15 minutes on a single consumer GPU.

Three contributions §I: the method; the SO-101 Task Set, 8 manipulation tasks in ManiSkill3 with heavy domain randomization; and real-world validation, with policies trained for 15 minutes and deployed zero-shot.

2SAC from the ground up

Squint is SAC with engineering. The fastest route to understanding it is to rebuild SAC one idea at a time, marking where Squint departs.

2.1The problem

The paper frames visual RL as a POMDP \(\langle\mathcal S,\mathcal O,\mathcal A,\mathcal T,R,\gamma\rangle\): the agent sees an observation, not the state, and seeks a policy that maximizes §III

\[\mathbb E_\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r_t\Big],\qquad r_t=R(s_t,a_t).\]

Derivation: what \(\gamma=0.9\) means in seconds

A reward \(k\) steps away is weighted \(\gamma^k\). All future weights sum to \(\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}=10\) steps, one second at 10 control steps per second. One second away: \(0.9^{10}=0.35\). Five seconds away, the end of an episode: \(0.9^{50}=0.005\).

Code

gamma: float = 0.9 L141. The paper does not state γ. (SAC's own table uses 0.99.)

Intuition

Squint's critic mostly values the next second. The dense, staged rewards of §4.2 pay at every step, so each stage of a five-second task has a reward inside that one-second view.

2.2Q-values and the Bellman equation

\(Q^\pi(s,a)\) is the expected discounted return after taking \(a\) in \(s\) and following \(\pi\) after. It satisfies a one-step recursion, which is what makes it learnable:

\[Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_{s'}\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb E_{a'\sim\pi}\,Q^\pi(s',a')\Big].\]

Temporal-difference learning fits a network \(Q_\theta\) to the right-hand side, the target \(y=r+\gamma\,Q(s',a')\). Using the network's own estimate inside its target is called bootstrapping.

Intuition

What I just got, plus what I believe comes next. One transition is enough to learn from. The price: errors in the belief feed back into the target, which is why the stabilizers of §2.5 exist.

2.3Off-policy learning and the replay buffer

The target needs a stored transition \((s,a,r,s')\) and a fresh next action \(a'\sim\pi(\cdot|s')\) from the current policy. It never asks which policy chose \(a\). So every transition ever collected stays useful: store them in a replay buffer \(\mathcal D\) and sample minibatches. That reuse is the sample efficiency. The paper's §I names the price: these gains come with computational costs that increase training wall-time.

2.4Continuous actions need an actor

With discrete actions the best action is \(\arg\max_a Q(s,a)\). With six continuous numbers there is no cheap argmax, so a second network, the actor \(\pi_\phi\), learns to output actions that score high on \(Q\), by following the critic's slope with respect to the action:

\[\nabla_\phi\,Q\big(s,\pi_\phi(s)\big)=\nabla_a Q(s,a)\big|_{a=\pi_\phi(s)}\,\nabla_\phi\pi_\phi(s).\]

This is the DDPG-style policy gradient; SAC's estimator extends the DDPG style policy gradients to any tractable stochastic policy SAC 2018a §4.2.

Derivation: how far the target lags

After \(k\) more updates, today's online weights carry weight \(\tau(1-\tau)^k\) in the target. That weight halves when \((1-\tau)^k=\tfrac12\): \(k=\frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln 0.99}=69\) updates, about a quarter of one iteration's 256.

Paper, §IV

We further investigate the effect of Clipped Double Q-learning (CDQ) and find a slight improvement in using the average rather than CDQ. TD3 had accepted the min's bias on purpose: its underestimation is far preferable to overestimation bias (TD3 §4.2). Squint's evidence for dropping it is its own ablation (Fig. 4).

2.6Maximum-entropy RL

SAC changes the objective itself: it pays the policy for staying random SAC 2018a §3.2:

\[J(\pi)=\sum_{t}\mathbb E_{(s_t,a_t)\sim\rho_\pi}\Big[r(s_t,a_t)+\alpha\,\mathcal H\big(\pi(\cdot|s_t)\big)\Big],\qquad \mathcal H(\pi(\cdot|s))=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}\big[-\log\pi(a|s)\big].\]

Values change to match SAC 2018b §4.1:

\[V(s)=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}\big[Q(s,a)-\alpha\log\pi(a|s)\big],\qquad \mathcal T^\pi Q(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb E_{s'}\big[V(s')\big].\]

And the policy improves by moving toward the Boltzmann distribution of its critic, projected back onto the family it can represent SAC 2018b §4.1:

\[\pi_{\text{new}}=\arg\min_{\pi'\in\Pi}\ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi'(\cdot|s)\,\middle\|\,\frac{\exp\big(\tfrac1\alpha Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s,\cdot)\big)}{Z(s)}\right).\]

SAC 2018a writes the same equations without α, as it can always be subsumed into the reward by scaling it by \(\alpha^{-1}\) (§3.2).

Derivation: why the ideal policy is \(\pi\propto e^{Q/\alpha}\)

At one state, maximize

\[\mathbb E_\pi[Q]+\alpha\mathcal H(\pi)=\sum_a\pi(a)\big(Q(a)-\alpha\log\pi(a)\big)\]

subject to \(\sum_a\pi(a)=1\). Setting the derivative of the Lagrangian to zero: \(Q(a)-\alpha\log\pi(a)-\alpha+\lambda=0\), so \(\pi(a)\propto\exp\big(Q(a)/\alpha\big)\). Substituting back, the value is \(V=\alpha\log\sum_a e^{Q(a)/\alpha}\): a soft maximum, which becomes \(\max_a Q(a)\) as \(\alpha\to0\).

Intuition

Do well, and stay as random as you can afford. The ideal policy is a distribution that puts more probability on better actions. \(\alpha\) is the exchange rate between reward and randomness; at \(\alpha\to0\) it is ordinary RL. SAC 2018a §3.2 lists the payoffs: the policy explores widely while giving up on clearly unpromising avenues, it can keep several modes of near-optimal behavior, and in their experiments it learned faster.

2.7SAC's three losses (the 2018b form Squint uses)

SAC 2018b drops the first paper's separate value network (its footnote: they later found it to be unnecessary) and learns the temperature. Three losses, three sets of weights.

Critic, the soft Bellman residual SAC 2018b §4.2; Squint Eq. 1–2:

\[J_Q(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\tfrac12\big(Q_\theta(s,a)-y\big)^2\Big],\] \[y=r+\gamma\Big(Q_{\bar\theta}(s',\tilde a')-\alpha\log\pi_\phi(\tilde a'|s')\Big),\qquad \tilde a'\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|s').\]

Squint's Eq. 2 omits the ½, which changes nothing about the minimizer.

Actor: the KL of §2.6, multiplied by \(\alpha\) SAC 2018b §4.2; Squint Eq. 3:

\[J_\pi(\phi)=\mathbb E_{s\sim\mathcal D,\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal N}\Big[\alpha\log\pi_\phi\big(f_\phi(\epsilon;s)\,|\,s\big)-Q_\theta\big(s,f_\phi(\epsilon;s)\big)\Big].\]

Derivation: from the KL to the actor loss

\[\begin{aligned}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi\,\|\,e^{Q/\alpha}/Z\big)&=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}\big[\log\pi(a|s)-\tfrac1\alpha Q(s,a)\big]+\log Z(s),\\ \alpha\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}&=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}\big[\alpha\log\pi(a|s)-Q(s,a)\big]+\alpha\log Z(s).\end{aligned}\]

The last term does not depend on \(\phi\), so it drops. Writing \(a=f_\phi(\epsilon;s)\) lets the gradient pass through the sample (§2.8).

Temperature. SAC 2018b §5 recasts the goal: find a stochastic policy with maximal expected return that satisfies a minimum expected entropy constraint, a floor \(\bar{\mathcal H}\) on the average entropy. \(\alpha\) is the dual variable of that constraint, trained with SAC 2018b §6

\[J(\alpha)=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}\big[-\alpha\log\pi(a|s)-\alpha\bar{\mathcal H}\big].\]

Derivation: why that loss moves \(\alpha\) the right way

Take \(\hat{\mathcal H}=-\log\pi(a|s)\), a one-sample estimate of entropy. Then \(J=\alpha(\hat{\mathcal H}-\bar{\mathcal H})\) and \(\partial J/\partial\alpha=\hat{\mathcal H}-\bar{\mathcal H}\). Gradient descent: \(\alpha\leftarrow\alpha-\lambda(\hat{\mathcal H}-\bar{\mathcal H})\). If the policy is less random than the target (\(\hat{\mathcal H}\lt\bar{\mathcal H}\)), \(\alpha\) rises, randomness pays more, and entropy climbs back. If it is too random, \(\alpha\) falls.

Code

Squint learns \(\log\alpha\) starting at 0, so \(\alpha\) starts at 1.0 L659–660; the argument alpha = 0.2 is used only when auto-tuning is off L135–137, L663. The loss (-log_alpha.exp() * (log_pi + target_entropy)).mean() L799 equals \(\alpha(\hat{\mathcal H}-\bar{\mathcal H})\): the same rule. The target is \(\bar{\mathcal H}=-\dim\mathcal A=-6\) L658, SAC 2018b's own choice: \(-\dim(\mathcal A)\) (e.g., −6 for HalfCheetah-v1) (its hyperparameter table).

Check: ran the code's loss

With \(\log\pi=+10\) (too sharp: \(\hat{\mathcal H}=-10\lt-6\)) the gradient on \(\log\alpha\) is −4, so descent raises \(\alpha\). With \(\log\pi=-10\) (too random) it is +16, so descent lowers \(\alpha\). Correct. The paper's Algorithm 1 prints the opposite sign (§8.1).

2.8The tanh-Gaussian policy

The actor outputs a mean \(\mu_\phi(s)\) and a log standard deviation for each of the 6 action numbers. A sample is noise pushed through the network, so gradients flow through it (the reparameterization trick, SAC 2018a §4.2), then squashed into \((-1,1)\):

\[u=\mu_\phi(s)+\sigma_\phi(s)\odot\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad a=\tanh(u).\]

Squashing changes the density. The Jacobian \(\mathrm d a/\mathrm d u=\mathrm{diag}(1-\tanh^2 u)\) is diagonal, so SAC 2018a App. C

\[\log\pi(a|s)=\log\mathcal N\big(u;\mu,\sigma^2\big)-\sum_{i=1}^{6}\log\big(1-\tanh^2(u_i)\big).\]

The code's log-likelihood against the exact density of \(\tanh(u)\) at four points: largest difference \(2.3\times10^{-5}\), all from the \(10^{-6}\) inside the log.

Check: what −6 asks for, and why wider is not more random

Through the squash, the entropy of \(a\) at \(\mu=0\) peaks at \(\sigma\approx0.88\) (0.684 nats, just under a uniform distribution's \(\log2=0.693\)) and then falls: wider Gaussians pile their samples against ±1. At \(\log\sigma=2\), 80% of samples land beyond ±0.95 and the entropy is −7.1 nats. Squint's target of −6 over 6 dimensions is −1 nat each, which at \(\mu=0\) means \(\sigma\approx0.09\) (\(\log\sigma=-2.41\)). Computed by numerical integration.

Intuition

tanh squeezes the whole real line into \((-1,1)\). Without the correction the policy would score its entropy bonus on the Gaussian, not on the actions it actually takes. Try the presets below.

2.9A critic that predicts a distribution (C51)

Instead of one number, the critic predicts a probability for each of \(N\) fixed return values, the atoms Bellemare et al. 2017 §4.1:

\[z_k=V_{\min}+k\,\Delta z,\qquad \Delta z=\frac{V_{\max}-V_{\min}}{N-1},\] \[p_k(s,a)=\operatorname{softmax}_k\big(\theta(s,a)\big),\qquad Q(s,a)=\sum_k p_k\,z_k.\]

Squint: \(N=101\) atoms on \([-20,20]\), so \(\Delta z=0.4\) L147–151. The name comes from the original paper's 51-atom agent: from here onwards, C51 Bellemare et al. 2017 §5.2.

The Bellman update moves every atom, \(\hat{\mathcal T}z_j=r+\gamma z_j\). The moved atoms land between the fixed ones, so each one's probability is split between its two neighbours in proportion to closeness Bellemare et al. 2017 §4.2:

\[\big(\Phi\hat{\mathcal T}Z\big)_i=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\left[1-\frac{\big|[\hat{\mathcal T}z_j]_{V_{\min}}^{V_{\max}}-z_i\big|}{\Delta z}\right]_0^1 p_j(s',a').\]

The loss is the cross-entropy of the predicted distribution against that projected target \(m\): \(-\sum_i m_i\log p_i(s,a)\).

Derivation: the projection keeps the mean

Atom \(j\) lands at \(b_j=(\hat{\mathcal T}z_j-V_{\min})/\Delta z\) with neighbours \(l=\lfloor b_j\rfloor\), \(u=l+1\), receiving weights \(u-b_j\) and \(b_j-l\). Its contribution to the mean:

\[\begin{aligned}p_j\big[(u-b_j)z_l+(b_j-l)z_u\big]&=p_j\big(V_{\min}+\Delta z\,[(u-b_j)\,l+(b_j-l)\,u]\big)\\&=p_j\big(V_{\min}+\Delta z\,b_j\big)=p_j\,\hat{\mathcal T}z_j.\end{aligned}\]

So the mean of the projected target equals the mean of the moved atoms (after clipping to the support). The explorer below shows both means.

Code: the soft version

Squint folds the entropy bonus into the reward before shifting: the moved atoms are \(\big(r-\gamma\alpha\log\pi(\tilde a'|s')\big)+\gamma z_j\) L417, L762–763, the critic target of §2.7 applied atom by atom.

Paper, §IV

We find that the choice of a distributional critic greatly improves wall time speed, even though it requires more computation than a standard Critic. The paper does not test why. Bellemare et al. 2017 discuss it in §6.1, Why does learning a distribution matter?

Check: when a moved atom lands exactly on the grid

In the original Algorithm 1, if \(b_j\) is a whole number then floor = ceil and both weights are zero: that atom's probability is dropped. With \(r=0,\gamma=1\), on Squint's float32 support, 74 of the 101 atoms land exactly, and the literal rule keeps only 26.7% of a uniform distribution. Squint shifts one index when \(b_j\) is whole L424–426 and keeps all of it in the same test. But its second line reuses a flag computed before the first line changed lower, so a hit at exactly \(b_j=1\) is counted twice. On a test support where \(\Delta z=1\) is exact (−50 to 50), Squint's own categorical() returned a total probability of exactly \(1+p_j\) in both cases tried (1.0194 and 1.0209). On the default support this needs a target landing exactly 0.4 above \(V_{\min}\) in float32; in the \(r=0,\gamma=1\) test, \(b_1\) came out as 0.99999905, not 1. An edge case, not a measured training problem. The fix is one line: test upper == lower again.

2.10Learning from pixels: who trains the encoder

We follow DrQ and DrQ-v2 in encoding \(\mathbf o_t\) with a single shared CNN encoder \(f_\psi\) ... The encoder \(f_\psi\) is trained end-to-end by the critic gradients §III; The encoder is only updated by the critic's TD-loss §IV.

Code

The critic's optimizer holds the critic and encoder weights; the actor's holds only the actor L717–720. The actor receives the features from the critic step, detached L812, L817.

Intuition

The encoder learns whatever features help predict returns. The actor reads those features but cannot reshape them.

Paper, §IV

An honest note from the authors: the shared encoder and the separate one-layer projections follow prior state-of-the-art visual RL methods, though our ablations reveal they yield negligible performance gains on our task set.

2.11Update-to-data ratio

UTD = gradient updates per transition collected. Squint: \(256/1024=0.25\) §IV; L85, L121. A related number, samples drawn per transition collected: \(256\times512/1024=128\).

Paper, §IV and §VII

Prior wall-clock work used a very low UTD ratio. The paper's reading of its own sweep: while very low UTD ratios (<0.06) work for humanoids, manipulation domains seem to benefit from larger UTD ratios, seemingly (0.25) in our task set (§IV). §VII shows higher UTD also helps state-based SAC on these tasks (Fig. 7).

Intuition

The simulator hands over 1,024 fresh transitions per tick. The UTD is how hard you study before the next tick arrives.

2.12LayerNorm

For a layer's \(H\) summed inputs \(a_i\) Ba et al. 2016 §3, §3.1:

\[\mu=\frac1H\sum_{i=1}^{H}a_i,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\frac1H\sum_{i=1}^{H}(a_i-\mu)^2},\qquad h=f\Big(\frac{g}{\sigma}\odot(a-\mu)+b\Big),\]

with learned gain \(g\) and bias \(b\) (PyTorch's nn.LayerNorm also adds \(10^{-5}\) under the square root). The statistics come from one example's layer, not from the batch.

Paper, §IV

All our linear layers are followed by Layer Normalization layers, which improve training speed, similar to the findings of RLPD (Ball et al. 2023), BRO (Nauman et al. 2024) and FastSAC.

Code

Every hidden linear layer is followed by LayerNorm: both projections L268, L271, the actor trunk L286–288, the critic trunk L376–378. The output layers (mean, log σ, atom logits) are not L290–291, L379, and neither are the convolutions.

3.1The loop

  1. Observe. Each of 1,024 environments renders a 128×128 wrist image, averages it down to 16×16, then colour jitter is applied L599–604.
  2. Act. While the step counter is below 5,000, uniformly random actions: 5 iterations, 5,120 transitions L876–877. After that, a sample from \(\pi_\phi\) L879; exploration comes only from the stochastic policy.
  3. Step. All environments advance one control step: 0.1 s of robot time, 10 physics steps at 100 Hz base_random_env.py L148.
  4. Store. Each \((o,s,a,r,o',s')\) goes into a first-in-first-out buffer of 1M transitions on the GPU L899–908, L732; §IV.
  5. Learn. 256 times: sample 512 transitions; update critic and encoder; update \(\alpha\); on every 4th pass update the actor; move the targets by \(\tau=0.01\) L914–929.
  6. Evaluate every 100,000 steps on 16 environments with the deterministic action \(\tanh(\mu)\), and save a checkpoint L860–873, L97, L87.

3.2Squinting: the idea in the name

Paper, §IV

Rendering at 128×128 and area-downsampling to 16×16 (squinting) beats rendering at 16×16 directly: it provides natural anti-aliasing and preserves scene structure. Across resolutions, the paper finds identical sample efficiency, but varying wall time: the smaller, the faster.

Code

F.interpolate(rgb.float(), size=(16, 16), mode='area').to(torch.uint8) utils.py L38. Checked: for 128 → 16 this equals the mean of each 8×8 block exactly, then truncation to an integer.

Derivation: why averaging anti-aliases

Each policy pixel is the mean of 64 rendered pixels, so a thin edge covering 10 of them contributes 10/64 of its colour. A renderer without anti-aliasing decides each pixel from one sample point instead: the edge is either fully in or out, and it flickers as the arm moves by sub-pixel amounts.

3.4The critic step, exactly

With \(\tilde a'\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|z',s')\) and its log-likelihood computed without gradients, for each head \(i\in\{1,2\}\):

\[t_j=\big(r-\gamma\,\alpha\log\pi_\phi(\tilde a'|z',s')\big)+\gamma z_j,\qquad m^{(i)}=\Phi\big(t;\ \bar p^{(i)}(z',s',\tilde a')\big),\] \[\mathcal L=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb E_{\text{batch}}\Big[-\sum_k m^{(i)}_k\log p^{(i)}_k(z,s,a)\Big].\]

Gradients flow into both heads, the critic's projection and the encoder, under bfloat16 autocast L751–793. The next-state features \(z'\) come from the online encoder under no-grad; there is no target encoder L753–756, L697–702.

Check: per-head targets, not an average

The paper's Algorithm 1, which says it omits the distributional critic, builds one target from the average of the two target critics and trains both heads on it. The code builds head \(i\)'s target from target head \(i\) alone; nothing mixes across heads in the critic step L410–449, L765–781. The averaging happens only in the actor step.

3.5The actor and temperature steps

Actor, every 4th critic step: a reparameterized \(\tilde a\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|\mathrm{sg}(z),s)\), the critic's weights frozen but its slope with respect to \(\tilde a\) kept L814–825, L393–408:

\[\mathcal L_\pi=\mathbb E\Big[\alpha\log\pi_\phi(\tilde a|z,s)-\tfrac12\big(Q_{\theta_1}(z,s,\tilde a)+Q_{\theta_2}(z,s,\tilde a)\big)\Big],\qquad Q_{\theta_i}=\sum_k p^{(i)}_k z_k.\]

Temperature, after every critic step, using the same batch's features from just before that step: \(\mathcal L_\alpha=-\alpha\big(\log\pi+\bar{\mathcal H}\big)\) L795–806.

3.6Episode ends: always bootstrap

bootstrap_at_done = "always" stores done = False for every transition L139, L888–890, and the episode's true final observation replaces the reset one L895–897. Terminations are ignored, so episodes end only at the 50-step limit L623, and all 1,024 end and restart together.

Intuition

A time limit is a rule of the training setup, not of the task: the world does not end at step 50. Treating it as an ending would teach the critic that value drops to zero at an arbitrary moment. Pardo et al. 2018 §3 give exactly this rule: target \(r\) at environmental terminations, \(r+\gamma\hat v(s')\) otherwise (including timeouts).

4.2Rewards, term by term

All four shape distance as \(1-\tanh(5d)\): 1 at contact, 0.54 at 10 cm, 0.24 at 20 cm. ManiSkill's default reward mode is normalized_dense, which divides each task's reward by its maximum, so a success step is worth 1.

Task (÷ max)Reward, in the order the code assigns it
Reach (÷4)\(1-\tanh(5d_{\text{goal}})\) + gripper closedness (0 to 1) + 1 if reached; set to 4 on success; then −3 if touching the table, −1 if touching the object
Lift (÷3)\(1-\tanh(5d_{\text{obj}})\) + 1 if grasped + \(e^{-2\lVert q_{\text{target}}-q_{\text{start}}\rVert}\) if grasped; −3 table; −1 while not lifted. No success bonus
Place (÷9)reaching \(2(1-\tanh 5d)\). Grasped: replaced by 3 + place terms (≤ 5). Object's centre over the bin: replaced by 4 + place terms + 1 if released + gripper openness + arm stillness (≤ 8 while held, ≤ 9 once released). Success: 9. Then −6 table, −3 bin, −1 while not lifted
Stack (÷9)reaching \(2(1-\tanh 5d)\). Grasped: 3 + place terms. On B, still touching A: 4 + place terms + openness. On B, released: 7 + the mean of A's and the arm's stillness. Success: 9. Then −6 table, −1 while not lifted

Place terms: \(1-\tanh(5d)\) to the resting goal, plus \(1-\tanh(10\,d_z)\). Place's height target is the resting height plus the bin wall plus 3 cm until the object is within the bin's half-diagonal sideways, then the resting height; Stack's is 3 cm above the resting height on B until within 4 cm. Stillness is \(1-\tanh(10v)\). Code: each task's compute_dense_reward.

Intuition

Staircases. Each stage pays more than the last, the success stage is a flat top that pays every step, and the −1 for an object still on the table makes dawdling expensive. With a one-second horizon (§2.1), the staircase is what pulls the agent through a five-second task.

Check: what these tests leave out

Grasping needs only 0.5 N per jaw, and nothing pays for squeezing harder; the simulated cube weighs 2.1 to 4.4 g (2.2 to 2.8 cm at 200 kg/m³) and the jaws have friction 2.0 so101.py L30. Place's success test has no height or object-speed condition, so it can register at the moment of release while the cube is still falling, and it measures the footprint along world axes even though the bin is rotated.

4.3What goes in and out

In: the 16×16 image, plus 12 numbers: the 6 joint angles with fresh Gaussian noise (σ = 5°) at every step, and the controller's 6 current joint targets reach.py L314–324. Out: 6 numbers in \([-1,1]\), each scaled to a change of at most 0.1 rad (arm) or 0.2 rad (gripper) and added to the controller's previous target, not to the measured angle (pd_joint_target_delta_pos, so101.py L97–109).

6.4Design choices and timing (what the text says)

The design-choice figure (Fig. 4, 5 seeds × 8 tasks) does not give its values in the text, so only the paper's stated conclusions are listed (§IV unless noted):

  • Batch 512 gives the same efficiency as larger batches at less wall time per update.
  • The distributional critic greatly improves wall time speed; the mean of two critics is slightly better than their minimum.

  • LayerNorm improves training speed.

Derivation: how fast the arm moves

In simulation: up to 0.1 rad per step × 10 steps/s = 57.3°/s per arm joint. On the paper's robot: 0.1 × 0.15 × 30 = 25.8°/s. On ours at 15 Hz: 12.9°/s. The policy's input has no clock (an image and 12 joint numbers), so a slower arm just takes more steps to arrive.

Paper, Appendix: the limitations the authors list

Visual robustness; sample efficiency; gripper design; single-task only (multi-task, multi-view and multi-agent left open); a symmetric critic that sees the same inputs as the actor (privileged critics left open); more and larger benchmarks; imitation learning under active vision; co-training with real data.

8Reading it critically

8.1Inconsistencies I found

1. Algorithm 1's temperature update has the wrong sign

It reads \(\alpha\leftarrow\alpha-\nabla_\alpha\frac1{|B|}\sum(\mathcal H^{\text{target}}-\mathcal H)\cdot\alpha\). The gradient is \(\mathcal H^{\text{target}}-\mathcal H\): when the policy is too sharp (\(\mathcal H\lt\mathcal H^{\text{target}}\)) it is positive, so \(\alpha\) falls and the policy gets sharper still. The released code minimizes \(-\alpha(\log\pi+\bar{\mathcal H})\), which is correct SAC (§2.7, checked by running it). The pseudocode is wrong; the code is right.

2. Algorithm 1 averages the critic targets; the code does not

Algorithm 1 trains both critics on \(y=r+\frac\gamma2\sum_i\big(Q_{\bar\theta_i}-\alpha\log\pi\big)\). The code trains head \(i\) on a distribution built from target head \(i\) only (§3.4). The pseudocode says it omits the distributional critic, so this may be a simplification, but the two are different estimators.

3. An edge case in the C51 projection

The index fix double-counts an atom landing exactly on grid index 1 (§2.9, shown with Squint's own function on an exactly representable support). Unlikely on the default support; a one-line fix.

4. "Around the gripper's starting position", approximately

The paper places objects in a 0.2m x 0.2m xy box around the gripper's starting xy position (§V). The code centres the box at (0.30, 0) m from the robot's base origin (spawn_box_pos in every task). In Squint's model at the start pose, the gripper tip sits at (0.23, −0.04) m, so the box is centred about 7 to 8 cm further out. A wording issue, not an error.

5. Smaller wording gaps

All our linear layers are followed by Layer Normalization: the output layers are not (§2.12). Place's success test uses world axes for a rotated bin and has no height test (§4.2).

What we learned beyond the paper

  • The camera matters exactly as §7 warns.   • A kinematics lead. At one grasp, our only measurement so far, the simulator's forward kinematics put the gripper tip 9.6 cm ahead of where the real gripper was. At the start pose, the camera's estimate of one ruler-measured cube was 1 to 2 cm off, and it creeps forward as the arm moves. Suspected cause: joint-calibration offsets (not yet proven). The policy still reached and grasped, we think because it steers by the image.
  • The release incentive. Place pays at most 5 while carrying, up to 8 the moment the cube's centre is over the bin while held, and 9 at success (§4.2). Our early releases fell just beside the near edge, consistent with that incentive plus the position error (hypothesis).
  • The grasp is a touch. While holding, the policy's gripper target stayed within about 0 to 3° of the jaws' actual angle: it never learned to squeeze, which §4.2's reward explains: nothing pays for a harder grip (the real cube's mass and the jaw friction are unmeasured).

10Teach it

10.1The paper in 60 seconds

Say this

Robots learn fastest in simulation, and the fastest simulators run thousands of robots on one GPU. The standard learner there, PPO, trains on each batch of experience and then throws it away. SAC keeps its experience and reuses it, but it is usually slow in minutes, and slower still from images. Squint keeps SAC and removes the bottlenecks one by one: a thousand simulated arms, a few hundred updates per step, a critic that predicts a distribution of returns, LayerNorm in every hidden layer, and images rendered sharp then averaged down to 16 by 16 pixels, so a million of them fit on the GPU. Fifteen minutes on one consumer GPU, eight manipulation tasks, and 73 of 80 real-robot trials with no real-world training data.
