1The game

What does the robot see, what does it do, and what does it get back?

In simulation the arm acts ten times a second. Each of those ticks it gets a snapshot of the world and has to answer with a move. Then the simulator tells it how good that moment was. RL papers call a tick a time step; section 7 sorts out the other things the code calls a step.

It seesa 16 × 16 colour picture from the camera on its wrist, and 12 numbers: its 6 joint angles and the 6 joint targets (where each joint has been told to go)
It does6 numbers between −1 and 1, one per joint: how far to move that joint's target this tick. All the way to 1 means 0.1 radian, about 6°; the gripper gets twice that
It getsa reward: one number that scores the moment, at most 1 per tick

Our running example is ReachCube: bring the gripper to the cube, close it, hold still. Its reward adds up how close the gripper is (0 to 1), how closed it is (0 to 1), and 1 more once it's at the cube. Success, meaning at the cube, closed and still, is worth 4; touching the table costs 3. Everything is divided by 4, so one tick is worth at most 1.

In the simulated run on this page the rewards went 0.25, 0.29, 0.33, 0.37, 0.42, 0.70, 0.73, and from tick 7 on, 1.00 every tick: success, held. An episode lasts 50 ticks, five simulated seconds, then the scene resets.

Four words carry the rest of the page:

  • observation (or state): what it sees
  • action: what it does
  • reward: the score for one tick
  • policy: its habit, the rule that turns what it sees into what it does

Nobody shows the robot what to do. The only teacher is the reward, and learning means changing the policy so that more reward comes in. That is reinforcement learning.

Under the hood: the reward in code

The ReachCube reward, exactly as trained (Squint fork at 7145d47, envs/reach.py L374–400):

The 12 numbers are noisy_qpos (in simulation, each joint reading gets 5° of random noise so the policy doesn't over-trust it) followed by the controller's 6 targets (envs/place.py L413–423). Each action is added to the previous target: target ← target + 0.1·a (envs/robot/so101.py L96–109). Simulation runs at 100 Hz with the policy acting at 10 Hz (envs/base_random_env.py L155); episodes are 50 ticks (reach.py L405).

2Keeping score

How do you judge a move whose payoff comes later?

Closing the gripper early earns little right away but makes success possible a few ticks later. So a move should be judged by everything that follows it, not by the next reward alone. Add up all the rewards from now on: that total is called the return.

One adjustment: rewards further in the future count a little less. Each tick further away is worth 0.9 times the one before. That factor is the discount, written γ (gamma). It keeps the total finite, and it makes sooner better than later.

return from tick t: G = r(t) + 0.9·r(t+1) + 0.9²·r(t+2) + 0.9³·r(t+3) + …

With γ = 0.9 the largest possible return is 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 + … = 10: full reward every tick, forever. Keep that 10 in mind. Every value on this page lives on that scale, and a robot holding a success sits near it.

Under the hood: why 10

A geometric series: 1 + γ + γ² + … = 1/(1 − γ). At γ = 0.9 that's 10, which also reads as "how many ticks ahead the robot effectively looks". A reward k ticks away is weighted γk: 0.35 at k = 10, 0.04 at k = 30. Squint sets gamma: float = 0.9 (train_squint.py L146), lower than the 0.99 common elsewhere, which suits short 50-tick tasks.

3The critic

How can the robot know a return before it has happened?

It learns to forecast it. The critic is a neural network that answers one question: if I'm here and make this move, then keep playing the way I usually do, what return should I expect? Its answer is called Q, for quality. (The same forecast without choosing the first move is called V.)

Think of the evaluation bar on a chess engine. It reads the position and says who's ahead long before the game ends. The critic is the robot's evaluation bar, measured in reward.

The critic sees what the robot sees, plus the move being judged. Inside, the picture goes through a small camera network (the encoder: two convolution layers) and is squeezed to 50 numbers; the 12 joint numbers are spread out to 256; the 6-number move is attached; three more layers turn all of that into the forecast.

Under the hood: the critic's wiring 
# train_squint.py L392-396: the critic's forward pass
proj = self.proj(rgb_features, state)       # picture -> 50 numbers, joints -> 256
x = torch.cat([proj, actions], dim=-1)      # attach the 6-number move
return torch.vmap(self._vmap_q, (0, None))(self.q_params, x)   # two heads, see section 8

Sizes from our checkpoint: encoder 34,400 weights; critic 1,536,096 (both heads, each three layers of 512 with layer normalization, train_squint.py L377–384). The forecast on this chart is the average of the two heads. "Actual" is the discounted sum of the rewards that came next, including SAC's small randomness bonus from section 6, because that is what this critic is trained to forecast.

4How the critic learns

How do you train a forecaster of the future?

The obvious way is to wait. Play the episode out, add up what actually happened, and correct the forecast toward that. It works (it's called Monte Carlo), but it's slow, because you wait for the end, and noisy, because one episode's luck decides the answer (Finn, slide 19).

The trick reinforcement learning uses instead: the return from now equals the reward you get now, plus 0.9 times the return from the next tick. After one tick you already know the first part. For the second part, ask the critic itself about the next tick. That gives a target that contains one more real reward than the forecast did (Finn, slide 18):

target = reward now + 0.9 × critic's forecast for the next tick

Then nudge the forecast a little toward the target. This is called bootstrapping, or temporal-difference learning. Here is one real step from our run:

Each target holds one real reward. Repeat this over millions of moments and the real rewards seep backward through the chain of forecasts, from the ticks that earn them to the moves that set them up.

Notice the catch, though: the target is built from the critic's own guess. If that guess is wrong, so is the target. Section 8 is about exactly this.

Under the hood: the target, exactly
y = r + γ · ( Q̄(s′, ã′) − α · log π(ã′ | s′) ) with ã′ drawn fresh from the actor at s′

Q̄ is the slow copy (section 8.1), the α·log π term is the randomness bonus (section 6), and in Squint the forecast is a histogram (section 9.1), so the same formula is applied to every bar. In code (train_squint.py L765–776, L422):

next_state_actions, next_state_log_pi, _ = actor.get_action(next_obs, next_state)
entropy_bonus = alpha * next_state_log_pi.flatten()
rewards_with_entropy = rewards - bootstrap.flatten() * discount * entropy_bonus
target_distributions = critic_target.categorical(next_obs, next_state, next_state_actions,
                                                 rewards_with_entropy, bootstrap, discount)
# inside categorical():
target_z = rewards.unsqueeze(1) + bootstrap.unsqueeze(1) * discount * self.q_support

Monte Carlo targets are unbiased but noisy; bootstrapped targets are steady but inherit the critic's errors (Finn, slides 19–20). Squint uses the 1-step version shown here.

5The actor

With a forecaster in hand, how does the robot pick a move?

If there were ten possible moves, you would forecast all ten and take the best. But a move is six numbers, each anywhere between −1 and 1: infinitely many choices. So a second network, the actor, proposes the move, and learns from the critic how to improve it.

The critic is a smooth function, so besides a forecast it can give a slope: move this joint a little this way and the forecast goes up by this much. The actor follows those slopes uphill. It's like hiking in fog: you can't see the summit, but you can feel which way the ground rises.

Two details make this work. First, the actor's move is built as center + spread × noise (section 6). Because the move is a smooth function of the center and the spread, the critic's slope passes straight through to them; this is called the reparameterization trick (Finn, slide 34). Second, the actor practises on situations from memory (section 7) but never copies the moves stored there. For each situation it proposes a fresh move and asks the critic to score that (Finn, slide 32).

the actor wants: Q(s, a) − α · log π(a | s) to be large, with a drawn fresh from the actor
Under the hood: the actor in code 
# train_squint.py L324-336: sample a move
mean, log_std = self.forward(rgb, state, get_log_std=True)
normal = torch.distributions.Normal(mean, log_std.exp())
x_t = normal.rsample()                    # center + spread * noise: slopes flow through
y_t = torch.tanh(x_t)                     # squash into [-1, 1]
log_prob = normal.log_prob(x_t)
log_prob -= torch.log(self.action_scale * (1 - y_t.pow(2)) + 1e-6)   # correct for the squash

# train_squint.py L828-834: the actor's loss
pi, log_pi, _ = actor.get_action(obs, state)
q_values = critic.get_q_values(obs, state, pi, detach_critic=True)
critic_value = q_values.mean(dim=0)       # average of the two heads
actor_loss = (alpha * log_pi - critic_value).mean()

detach_critic=True freezes the critic during this step: gradients move the actor's move, not the critic's opinion. The slope in the figure is this gradient, measured at the actor's deterministic move. The older policy-gradient form, ∇log π · Q (Finn, slides 32–33), estimates the same direction with much more noise.

6Random on purpose

Why would a learner act randomly, and how random should it be?

An actor that always plays its current best guess never finds out about better moves it hasn't tried. So the actor doesn't output one move. For each joint it outputs a center and a spread, a bell curve, and the move is a random draw from it.

The draw can land anywhere, but moves must stay between −1 and 1, so Squint bends the bell with a squashing function, tanh. A center near 0 stays bell-shaped; a center far out piles the draws up against the edge.

real settings:

How much randomness is right? SAC answers by paying the actor for it. The actor's goal becomes reward plus α times the entropy, a measure of how spread out its choices are. α, the temperature, is the exchange rate between reward and randomness.

α isn't set by hand. A thermostat adjusts it toward a target amount of randomness: entropy −6, one unit per joint, roughly a spread of 0.09 per joint near the middle. If the actor is more random than that, α shrinks; if it's less, α grows. In our run α started at 1 and settled at .

The actor gets to choose where to spend that randomness. By tick 30, holding at the cube, the arm joints' spread was , but the gripper's was . At the hold the forecast barely changes with the gripper move, so randomness there costs nothing (our reading of the trace).

When the trained policy runs on the real arm, the randomness is switched off. It plays the center of each bell, squashed: tanh(center).

Under the hood: the entropy objective and the thermostat
actor's objective: E[ Q(s, a) ] + α · H(π(· | s)), H = E[ −log π(a | s) ] thermostat: minimize −α · ( log π(a | s) + H_target ), H_target = −6
# train_squint.py L667-670, L807-808
target_entropy = -torch.prod(torch.Tensor(envs.unwrapped.single_action_space.shape)).item()   # -6
log_alpha = torch.zeros(1, requires_grad=True)        # alpha starts at exp(0) = 1
_, log_pi, _ = actor.get_action(obs, state)
alpha_loss = (-log_alpha.exp() * (log_pi + target_entropy)).mean()

If the actor is too sure, log π is large, (log π − 6) > 0, and the gradient raises α, paying more for randomness; too random, and α falls. The "spread of 0.09" is the Gaussian whose entropy is −1: ½·ln(2πe·σ²) = −1 gives σ ≈ 0.089, before squashing. The spread is also capped: log σ is kept between −5 and 2 (train_squint.py L306–307). At deployment the actor plays torch.tanh(mean) (L320–323).

7Memory and the loop

Where does the learning data come from, what exactly is stored, and how often is it used?

Squint runs 1024 simulated arms at once on one GPU. Every tick, each arm adds one moment to a shared memory, which keeps the last 1,000,000 of them. It's called the replay buffer.

One moment, up close

Each entry in memory is one tick of one arm, written (s, a, r, s′): what the arm saw, what it did, what that earned, and what it saw next. In simulation one tick unfolds like this:

sthe 16 × 16 picture, after colour jitter, and the 12 joint numbers at the start of the tick
athe 6 numbers the actor chose, between −1 and 1; the controller turns them into target changes of at most 0.1 rad
rone number, at most 1: the reward for the state the tick ended in
s′the picture and 12 numbers at the end of the tick, which are also the next tick's s
donealways "no" in Squint, because a time limit isn't an ending (section 9.2)

One moment takes about 1.7 KB, so a million fit in 1.7 GB of GPU memory. With 128 × 128 pictures, the same million would need about 98 GB.

The tick is where the action frequency comes in. In simulation the policy acts 10 times a second, so s and s′ are 0.1 s apart, and in that time a joint can move at most 0.1 rad, about 6°. The discount counts in ticks too: "10 ticks ahead" means one second. The policy never sees a clock, only pictures and numbers, so it has learned how much the world changes between s and s′ at this pace. On our real arm it acts 15 times a second and every move is scaled to 15%, so a tick moves a joint at most 0.86° instead of 5.7°: under a quarter of the training speed.

Four things called a step

NameWhat it isHow long, or how many
tick, or time stepone decision of one arm: see, act, get a reward0.1 s in simulation; 1/15 s on our real arm

iterationall 1024 arms take one tick, then the learner makes its updates1024 new moments; the code's global_step counts moments, so it jumps by 1024
update, or gradient stepone round of learning from 512 random moments256 per iteration

For scale: checkpoint A trained for 196 iterations, which is 200,704 moments, 50,176 updates, and 19.6 simulated seconds per arm, about four episodes each. Its memory never filled. Checkpoint P, for PlaceCube, ran 1,465 iterations: 1.5 million moments and 375,040 updates, so its memory filled and the oldest half-million moments were dropped.

How often each moment is used

Each iteration's 256 updates draw 256 × 512 = 131,072 moments at random, against 1024 new ones. So on average every moment is studied about 128 times.

Why keep old moments? A moment is a fact about the world: from here, this move led there and paid this much. It stays true after the actor changes, so the critic can keep learning from it. That's what "off-policy" means (Finn, slide 31). Drawing at random also breaks up runs of near-identical neighbouring ticks, which would otherwise pull the networks back and forth.

at the startabout 5,000 moments of random moves, to put something in memory every iteration1024 arms tick once; 1024 new moments go into memory; then 256 updates every updatecritic and camera network learn · α thermostat adjusts · slow copy drifts 1% (section 8) every 4th updateactor learns (section 8.3) every 50 ticksall 1024 arms reset together and start new episodes; success doesn't end an episode early

The paper found that manipulation likes this many updates per new moment (0.25), more than humanoid walking needs (under 0.06) (Squint, section IV).

Inside envs.step, ManiSkill runs sim_freq // control_freq = 100 // 10 = 10 physics steps, then evaluates the new state and computes the reward from it. The controller sets each joint's new target at once (no interpolation) and the simulated motors pull toward it during those 10 steps. Episodes all end together because the vector environment ignores early terminations (ignore_terminations=True, L632). Memory per moment: two 16 × 16 × 3 pictures (1,536 bytes) plus 12 + 12 + 6 + 1 numbers and a flag, about 1.7 KB; the memory lives on the GPU (LazyTensorStorage, L741). The paper found 1M moments beat 100K by 7% in final success.

8Why learning wobbles, and three steadiers

What goes wrong when two networks teach each other?

There are two feedback loops in what you've seen so far. The critic learns from its own forecasts (section 4). The actor chases the critic's slopes (section 5). Either loop can turn a small error into a big one. Three standard fixes each guard one weak spot.

8.1 A slow copy for the targets

The problem: the target is reward + 0.9 × the critic's forecast for the next tick. Every update changes the critic, so it also moves the target. It's like aiming at a target taped to your own arm: each correction moves it. Errors can chase themselves around and grow.

The fix: keep a slow copy of the critic and use it only to compute targets. The copy is never trained. After each update it moves 1% of the way toward the live critic (this rate is called τ, tau). The goalpost still moves, but slowly and smoothly.

A made-up signal, not our data. Thin line: the live critic's opinion over updates. Thick line: the slow copy.

In Squint, τ = 0.01, so a change in the critic takes 69 updates to reach halfway into the targets. The copy covers the critic's own layers; the camera network isn't copied, and targets use the live one. One more detail that matters for us: the checkpoint saves the slow copy, so the critic we used to score the real arm is the slow copy.

Under the hood: the moving average
θ̄ ← θ̄ + τ · (θ − θ̄) halfway after k = ln(0.5) / ln(1 − τ) updates; τ = 0.01 → 69

torch._foreach_lerp_(critic_target_params, critic_online_params, args.tau)   # tau = 0.01 (L148)

DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) froze a copy and replaced it every few thousand steps; SAC and TD3 use the smooth moving average above. The Squint paper doesn't test removing it.

8.2 Two critics against the optimist's curse

The problem: the actor goes wherever the critic forecasts high. Forecasts have errors, and the actor is drawn precisely to the ones that are accidentally too high. Those inflated values then feed into targets, and the inflation spreads.

You can see the bias without any robot. Give several moves the same true value, 0. Add noise to each forecast. Pick the move that looks best. Its forecast is above 0 on average, and more so with more moves to choose from.

A simulation of the idea, 4,000 draws: every move is truly worth 0.

The standard fix, from TD3 and then SAC: train two critics and build targets from the smaller of their two forecasts, which cancels much of the optimism.

Squint keeps two critic heads but does something milder. Each head learns from its own slow copy, and the actor follows the average of the two. The paper found the average slightly better than the minimum (Squint, section IV, Fig. 4).

Under the hood: per-head targets

...

# L787-790: each head trained against its own target, losses summed
q_losses = -torch.sum(target_distributions * q_log_probs, dim=-1).mean(dim=-1)

The minimum rule is TD3's "clipped double Q" (Fujimoto et al., 2018); TD3 accepted its slight pessimism on purpose. Squint drops it on the strength of its own ablation.

8.3 Let the critic settle before the actor listens

The problem: the actor learns from the critic's slopes. When the critic is wrong, the actor learns wrong moves; the wrong moves change the data, and the changed data confuses the critic further.

The fix, also from TD3: update the actor less often than the critic. Squint updates the actor on every 4th update (policy_frequency = 4, train_squint.py L136, L932). The paper doesn't test this choice on its own.

9What Squint adds

Everything so far is SAC. What does Squint change, and why?

The paper names its ingredients: many simulated arms in parallel, a histogram critic, squinting, layer normalization, more updates per moment, and a fast implementation (Squint, section IV). Parallel arms and updates per moment were section 7. Here are the rest.

9.1 A histogram instead of one number

Instead of saying "I expect 8.3", Squint's critic gives a chance for every possible return, in 101 slots from −20 to 20, each 0.4 apart. The forecast is the histogram's average.

The target is built just as in section 4, but for the whole shape: take the slow copy's histogram for the next tick, shrink it toward zero by 0.9, slide it by the reward, and split each bar between the two nearest slots. The critic is trained like a classifier, with cross-entropy, to match that shape.

That is tick 3 of our run again, now as shapes. At the real reward the target averages 8.33 and the critic's current forecast 8.27, so training pulls the bottom shape toward the middle one. The re-binning in this widget is the training code's, ported and checked against it.

The paper found this critic makes training much faster in wall-clock time, even though each update costs more (Squint, section IV). A shape also says how sure the critic is. Here is checkpoint P's critic on three frames from a real PlaceCube run on our arm:

Under the hood: the re-binning and the loss 
# train_squint.py L418-431 (C51, Bellemare et al., 2017)
target_z = (rewards + bootstrap * discount * self.q_support).clamp(self.v_min, self.v_max)
b = (target_z - self.v_min) / delta_z                # where each shifted bar lands, in slot units
lower, upper = torch.floor(b).long(), torch.ceil(b).long()
# bar mass p goes to the two nearest slots: p*(upper - b) to lower, p*(b - lower) to upper
# L784-787: cross-entropy against the target shape
q_log_probs = F.log_softmax(q_outputs, dim=-1)
q_losses = -torch.sum(target_distributions * q_log_probs, dim=-1).mean(dim=-1)

The forecast is Σ pj·zj over the 101 slots (L412–413). Because the slots stop at −20 and 20, forecasts can't run away (the clamp above).

9.2 Small things that keep numbers sane

Every layer in all three networks is followed by layer normalization, which rescales that layer's numbers to a standard size. With each moment studied over a hundred times, nothing then drifts to extremes. The paper found it speeds training up (section IV).

Episodes stop at 50 ticks because a clock runs out, not because the world ends. So every target keeps its "0.9 × next forecast" part, even at the last tick: a timer isn't treated as an ending (bootstrap_at_done = "always", train_squint.py L897–899).

9.3 Squinting

The policy sees 16 × 16 pictures, which makes the networks, the memory and every update small and fast. The paper found smaller pictures learn just as much per moment but train faster per minute (section IV).

The name is about how the small picture is made. Squint renders at 128 × 128 and averages each 8 × 8 block into one pixel, rather than rendering at 16 × 16 directly. Averaging smooths the picture and keeps the scene's shapes. The paper found it trains better, with real-world success similar to higher resolutions. The pictures in section 1 are exactly this.

Only the critic trains the camera network. The actor uses its output, but the actor's updates don't change it (following DrQ; the paper found little difference on its tasks). During training the colours are also jittered at random, brightness, contrast and saturation by up to 30% and hue by 5%, so that the policy cares less about exactly how the scene looks:

9.4 Why minutes, not hours

All 1024 arms run on one GPU; the memory sits on the GPU too, because the pictures are tiny; and the update code is compiled (PyTorch compile and CUDA graphs) and runs in 16-bit floats, which the paper reports as more than 5× faster together. Given 15 minutes of training, Squint averaged 96.1% success in simulation across its 8 tasks and 73 of 80 trials on a real SO-101 (Squint, Tables I and II).

10One update, start to finish

Now all the pieces at once. What happens in one of the 256 updates?

  1. Draw 512 random moments from memory: situation s, move a, reward r, next situation s′. L925
  2. Run all the pictures through the camera network. L763, L779
  3. The actor proposes a fresh next move a′ at s′, and reports how likely it was, log π(a′ | s′). L765
  4. The slow copy gives each head's histogram for (s′, a′). Shrink by 0.9, slide by r − 0.9·α·log π(a′ | s′), re-bin: that's the target. L771–776
  5. The critic and camera network take one step to bring their histogram for (s, a) closer to the target. L783–800
  6. The α thermostat takes one step toward entropy −6. L805–815
  7. On every 4th update, the actor proposes a fresh move at s and takes one step toward a higher average forecast plus randomness. L828–837
  8. The slow copy moves 1% toward the critic. L938

After 256 of these, all 1024 arms take their next tick, and it starts again.

11Common confusions

  • "The critic predicts the reward." It forecasts the return, all future reward, discounted. One tick's reward is at most 1; a return can reach 10.
  • "The actor learns from the moves in memory." Only the situations come from memory. The moves it's scored on are fresh from the current actor.
  • "The slow copy is a third network that learns." It's never trained. It's a lagging average of the critic.
  • "Two critics means twice the learning." They're there to temper the critic's mistakes. Squint averages them; standard SAC takes the smaller.
  • "Randomness is only for the beginning." SAC keeps a set level of randomness all through training. It's switched off only when the robot is deployed.
  • "A step is one thing." In this code it can mean one tick of one arm, one tick of all 1024 arms (which global_step counts as 1024), or one learning update. Section 7 has all four.
  • "Once trained, the critic is useless." Deployment leaves it behind, but it still knows how a run is going. We use it to grade real runs on our arm.
