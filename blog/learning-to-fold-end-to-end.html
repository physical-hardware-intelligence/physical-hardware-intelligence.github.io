1The job

What does the robot get, and what does it have to do?

Two SO-101 arms sit at a table with one piece of clothing lying flat between them. The job is to fold it. That was the task of the LeHome Challenge at ICRA 2026, with four kinds of clothes: long-sleeved tops, short-sleeved tops, long pants and shorts, all in children's sizes (report §1.1, §9.1).

Twenty times a second, the robot gets a snapshot of the world and has to answer with where its joints should go (report §1.1).

It seesthree pictures, one from above the table and one from each wrist, and 12 numbers: the angle of every joint, six per arm, in degrees. The gripper's number runs from 0, closed, to 100, open
It answers12 numbers: where each joint should go next

Who made it: this policy took first place of 62 teams in the simulation round and second in the real-robot final, 865 of 1,080 points (report §8.1, §9.12). This part explains how it works and what we changed to run it. How it got good in simulation is part 2.

Four words carry the rest of the page:

  • snapshot (papers say observation): what it sees at one moment
  • move (action): the 12 joint targets it answers with
  • policy: the rule that turns a snapshot into moves; here, the whole network
  • demonstration: a recording of a person doing the task
The mathsthe policy as a function

A snapshot is three pictures and the joint angles, \(o_t = (I^{\text{top}}_t, I^{\text{left}}_t, I^{\text{right}}_t, q_t)\) with \(q_t \in \mathbb R^{12}\). The policy maps it to a plan of 30 moves, of which the robot plays the first few (chapter 4):

\[ \pi:\; o_t \;\mapsto\; (\hat a_t, \hat a_{t+1}, \dots, \hat a_{t+29}) \in \mathbb R^{30 \times 12}. \]

Inside, the network does not predict joint targets directly. It predicts changes from where the arms are now, \(\hat a_{t+k} - q_t\), each of the 30 steps scaled by its own mean and spread, because a change 29 steps ahead is naturally much larger than one step ahead (report §4.6). The server adds \(q_t\) back before replying, so what reaches the robot are joint targets in degrees.

2Copy a person

Can't it just copy people?

The organisers recorded 500 folds by people driving the robot by hand through a second, matching pair of arms (teleoperation). That gave 187,135 snapshots, each paired with what the person did next (report §9.6, training-mix table).

The simplest way to make a robot fold is to copy them. Show the network a snapshot, ask "what did the person do next?", and nudge its answer toward what the person actually did. Repeat over every snapshot, many times. This is behaviour cloning, imitation learning in its plainest form, and the checkpoint we run was trained this way: no rewards and no trial and error on the real robot (report §9.5; the checkpoint's model card).

It has one catch. People don't all fold the same way. In Ilia's own training data the short-sleeved top was folded starting from one sleeve in the simulator recordings and from the other sleeve in the real ones (report §9.1), so snapshots that look alike come with two different right answers. Here is the smallest version of that problem: some people reach for the towel's left corner first, the rest for the right.

A toy, not our data: each person's first reach lands near the left corner (−1) or the right (+1), with a little scatter.

If the network has to give one answer, and is graded by how far that answer is from the person's (the usual squared distance), the best it can do is the average of what people did. With people split between two corners, the average is the middle of the edge, where nobody reached. Slide the split: copying lands on a corner only when everyone agrees.

Copying the average of two good moves gives a bad one. The robot needs a way to copy that picks one of the ways people do it, and commits.

The mathswhy squared error gives the average

For one snapshot, let \(a\) be the person's move and \(c\) the network's single answer. The squared error, averaged over the people, is

\[ L(c) = \mathbb E\big[(a - c)^2\big], \qquad L'(c) = -2\,\mathbb E[a - c] = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c^\star = \mathbb E[a], \]

and \(L''(c) = 2 > 0\), so that is the minimum. Over many snapshots the best network answers \(\mathbb E[a \mid o]\), the average move for that snapshot. With a share \(p\) going to \(-1\) and the rest to \(+1\):

\[ c^\star = -p + (1-p) = 1 - 2p, \qquad \text{distance to the nearest corner} = 1 - |1 - 2p|. \]

At \(p = \tfrac12\) the answer is 0, a full corner-width from both real moves. No bigger network fixes this: any network trained this way heads for the average. It needs a different question.

Check yourself70% of people go left, 30% right. Where does copying send the gripper?

To \(1 - 2(0.7) = -0.4\): on the left side, but still 0.6 short of the corner. Copying the average never commits until everyone agrees.

3Commit to one way

How does a network learn to commit to one way?

Stop asking for one answer; draw one instead. Start with a random move, pure noise, and nudge it toward the moves people made, a little at a time. Each nudge comes from the network, which has learned, for any half-made move, which direction leads toward something a person would have done. After ten nudges the random move has become one of the moves people made. Which one depends on where the noise started: different noise, a different but equally human move.

This is flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023), the same idea Physical Intelligence's π0 used for robot moves (Black et al., 2024), and it is how Ilia's model makes every plan (report §7.1). Watch it on the toy from chapter 2:

The toy from chapter 2, and the nudges are computed exactly for it, not learned. Each line is one draw: noise at the top, a move at the bottom.

Try one nudge. Every line lands on the same point, the average: exactly the answer plain copying gave. One big nudge can only aim at where people are on average. With more, smaller nudges, each line first drifts toward the middle and then gets pulled to whichever side it is nearer, and the lines split in the same proportions as the people. Ilia's model uses ten (report §7.1).

How it learns the nudges

Training runs the picture backwards. Take a real move a person made, \(a\), and some noise, \(z\). Blend them, \(x = t\,z + (1-t)\,a\), with \(t\) picked at random between 0 (all move) and 1 (all noise). Then ask the network, given the snapshot, the blend and \(t\): which way is the move? The right answer is the straight line from the noise to the move, \(z - a\). Do this for millions of snapshots, moves, noises and \(t\)'s, and the network learns the nudge for every point along the way. On the robot it starts from fresh noise at \(t = 1\) and takes ten steps of 0.1 back to \(t = 0\).

Where the starting noise comes from

The starting noise is one random number for every joint at every move of the plan: 12 joints times 30 moves (chapter 4 explains the 30), 360 numbers in all. The server keeps one random-number generator and takes a fresh draw for every request, so no two plans start from the same place.

The numbers are not picked independently. Picked independently, each joint would jump about from one move to the next, and no real arm moves like that. So Ilia shapes the draw with how real plans vary together, measured on the training recordings: there, a joint's value at one move is almost exactly its value at the next (a correlation of 0.999), and the joints move partly together. His draw keeps half of that shape and adds half plain independent noise, which brings neighbouring moves down to a correlation of 0.5, "for robustness" in the code's own words (report §1.3). The same kind of noise is used in training and on the robot. Last, our robot program turns it down a little: a noise temperature of 0.9 multiplies its variance by 0.9, so its size by about 0.95.

The mathsthe training target, the ten steps, and why one step gives the average

Training. With \(t \in (0, 1]\), noise \(z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\) and the person's plan \(a\),

\[ x_t = t\,z + (1-t)\,a, \qquad u = \frac{d x_t}{d t} = z - a, \qquad \mathcal L(\theta) = \mathbb E\,\big\| v_\theta(x_t, t, o) - u \big\|^2 . \]

As in chapter 2, a squared error is minimised by an average, here the average direction over every \((z, a)\) that could have produced the same blend: \(v^\star(x, t, o) = \mathbb E[\,z - a \mid x_t = x, o\,]\). Ilia draws \(t\) from a Beta(1.5, 1) distribution, which picks the noisy end more often than the clean one.

The ten steps are Euler's method on \(dx/dt = v\), run from \(t = 1\) to \(0\):

\[ x \leftarrow x - 0.1\, v_\theta(x, t, o), \qquad t \leftarrow t - 0.1, \qquad \text{ten times.} \]

Why one step gives the average. At \(t = 1\) the blend is pure noise, \(x_1 = z\), which says nothing about \(a\). So \(\mathbb E[z \mid x_1 = x] = x\) and \(\mathbb E[a \mid x_1 = x] = \mathbb E[a]\), and \(v^\star(x, 1) = x - \mathbb E[a]\). One step of size 1 gives \(x - v^\star(x, 1) = \mathbb E[a]\) for every starting point: the average from chapter 2. The later steps see blends that already lean to one side, and that is where the split happens.

The starting noise. Let \(\Sigma\) be the correlation of the 360 normalised plan numbers in the training recordings, and \(\beta = 0.5\). With \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I_{360})\) and \(L\) the Cholesky factor of the blend,

\[ \Sigma_{\text{used}} = \beta\,\Sigma + (1-\beta)\,I, \qquad \Sigma_{\text{used}} = L L^\top, \qquad z = \sqrt{\tau}\, L\, \varepsilon , \]

so \(z\) has covariance \(\tau\,\Sigma_{\text{used}}\). The training blend \(x_t\) uses the same \(L\) (with \(\tau = 1\)); our robot uses \(\tau = 0.9\).

What is proven, and what isn't. With the exact field and infinitely small steps, the draws follow the distribution of people's moves (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Ten finite steps of a learned field are an approximation. In the toy, ten are enough for two clean moves in the right proportions (checked below).

4Plan ahead, re-plan often

Why does each answer have five moves instead of one?

The organisers recorded at 20 moves a second, so the model thinks in moves of a twentieth of a second (report §1.1). Asking it takes time: on our rig about s from sending the pictures to getting the answer back. If every answer were a single move, the arm would wait that long for every twentieth of a second of motion.

So every answer is a plan for the next 30 moves, a second and a half of motion. The robot plays only the first 5, a quarter of a second, then asks again with fresh pictures (report §7.1). The long plan keeps the motion smooth; asking again every 5 moves lets the arm react to what the cloth does.

So in a second our arms get about moves, roughly half the 20 the model was trained on, because they stand still while each answer is on its way. It still folds. Asking for the next plan while the current one plays is one of part 3's levers.

Joining the plans

There's a trap. Every plan starts from fresh noise, and chapter 3 showed that fresh noise can pick the other way. Re-plan from scratch and the arm could change its mind every quarter second.

So each plan is tied to the last one. The robot keeps one move from just past the five it played, the anchor, and sends it with the next request. While the next plan is drawn, its first move is held on a path to the anchor during the first 4 of the 10 nudges, and the pull spreads to the rest of the plan through how joints usually move together. The last 6 nudges are free, so the plan can still sharpen and correct itself (report §7.2). Ilia's name for the effect is mode stickiness: the robot stays in the way of folding it already chose.

Each colour is one answer's 5 moves, and each one picks up where the last left off: across a whole fold the shoulder swings through about °, yet the plan never jumps between answers.

The mathsexecute, keep, and the soft anchor

Three numbers say how a plan of \(H = 30\) is used (report §7.1): the robot plays \(n_e = 5\) moves, keeps \(n_a = 1\) as the anchor, and stretches playback by \(k = 1\) (none), so the five moves take five control steps of \(1/20\) s. One cycle lasts the time to answer plus \(n_e/20 = 0.25\) s; in run 4 the mean cycle was s, so about \(5/0.46 \approx 11\) moves a second.

The joins are as smooth as the plan itself: in run 4, 95% of the jumps between one answer's last move and the next answer's first were under ° for the left shoulder lift, and 95% of the steps inside an answer were under °.

The anchor enters the drawing of the next plan. Flatten the plan to \(30 \times 12 = 360\) numbers; let \(O\) be the entries the anchor covers and \(z\) the starting noise. After each step to time \(t'\), the anchored entries are put back on the straight path from the noise to the anchor,

\[ x_O \leftarrow (1 - t')\,a^{\text{anchor}}_O + t'\,z_O , \]

and the change this made, \(\delta\), is passed on to the free entries through a fixed matrix \(C\) built from how actions vary together in the training data. That happens only while \(t' > 0.5\), which in the code's arithmetic is the first 4 of the 10 steps (checked below).

5From pixels to a plan

How does a network get from pixels to "the corner is there"?

Five hundred folds are far too few to learn to see from scratch. So the model is built from parts that already learned to see and to read, from enormous collections of pictures and text, plus a part that learns to move (report §1.3).

  1. Each 224-pixel picture is cut into a 16 × 16 grid of 14-pixel patches. SigLIP, a vision network trained to match pictures with their captions (Zhai et al., 2023), turns each patch into a token: a list of numbers describing it. That's 256 tokens per camera, 768 in all. SigLIP stays frozen: Ilia's training never changes it.
  2. Each of the 12 joint angles, scaled to lie between −1 and 1, is rounded into one of 256 bins and looked up as a token of its own (report §4.1).
  3. One token says which garment this is (chapter 6), and one more, the query, is a blank token where the model writes what it thinks of the scene.
  4. Gemma, a language model with 2 billion weights (Gemma Team, 2024), reads all 782 tokens together; each token can draw on the others (attention).
  5. The action expert, a smaller transformer with 0.43 billion weights and the same 18 layers, holds 30 tokens, one per move of the plan. It reads everything Gemma made of the snapshot and runs the ten nudges of chapter 3 on its 30 tokens.

One rule about who may look at whom matters later. The pictures and the query form the first group; the joints and the garment token come after. A later group can look at an earlier one, never the reverse (report §4.1). So the query sees pictures and nothing else.

The design is Physical Intelligence's π0.5 (2025), extended by Ilia's team for the BEHAVIOR-1K challenge in 2025, and then by Ilia for LeHome (report §1.3).

The mathswho sees whom, and the joint bins

Give every token a group number, as the code does: pictures and the query 0, joints and the garment token 1. In the reader, token \(i\) may attend to token \(j\) exactly when \(g(j) \le g(i)\). The 30 move tokens attend to every reader token and to each other (report §4.1).

A joint angle scaled to \(s \in [-1, 1]\) becomes the bin \(\min\!\big(255,\ \lfloor 128\,(s + 1) \rfloor\big)\): 256 equal bins, each 1/128 of the range wide.

The token count at inference: \(3 \times 256 + 1 + 12 + 1 = 782\). In training two more kinds of token appear, for rewards and for a second way of writing the moves; the real checkpoint switches the first off and never sends the second at inference (report §4.1, §9.5).

6Which garment is this?

Shirts and pants fold differently. How does it know which one is on the table?

Each garment type has its own learned token, read with the joints (report §4.2). Early on, Ilia saw the arms make the moves for one garment while the token named another, so the garment also gets a direct line into every layer of the action expert (AdaRMS conditioning, report §4.4). Between them, they steer which folding sequence the plan follows.

In training the token is always the true garment. On the robot nobody says what is on the table (report §1.1). So the model tells itself: a tiny classifier, 8,196 weights reading the query, picks one of the four types from the pictures alone. Late in Ilia's simulation training it was right more than 99% of the time (report §4.2).

The robot program uses it like this. The first request goes out with no garment token. That answer's plan is thrown away, the arms hold still, and its guess is kept. Every later request carries that guess, for the rest of the run (report §7.6). In simulation Ilia's client also took a majority vote over the first few answers (report §7.6); the real-robot program we run keeps only the first.

The towel isn't one of the four, and in all towel runs with a log, the first guess was a short-sleeved top. It folded anyway. Our adult boxer shorts look about one and a half times the size of the organisers' children's shorts (by eye), and there the first guess was right in 1 of 4 runs: . In run 2 it latched onto long pants and kept them for the whole run, while its own guesses said long top on 96 of 180 answers.

One picture decides the whole run. The obvious fix is letting the person at the table say what's on it; part 3, chapter 3 shows why, for our shorts, that alone isn't enough.

The mathsthe guess, its training, and the steering

Let \(h \in \mathbb R^{2048}\) be Gemma's output at the query. The garment head is one linear layer, \(\ell = W h + b\) with \(W \in \mathbb R^{4 \times 2048}\), \(b \in \mathbb R^{4}\): \(4 \times 2048 + 4 = 8{,}196\) weights. The guess is \(\arg\max_k \ell_k\). In real-robot training it learns by cross-entropy, \(-\log \operatorname{softmax}(\ell)_g\) for the true garment \(g\), weighted 0.1 next to the flow-matching loss.

The guess steers two ways. As a token \(e_g \in \mathbb R^{2048}\) in the reader, and inside the action expert, where every layer's normalisation is modulated by one conditioning vector \(c\). The flow time already sets \(c\); the garment adds its own learned vector (report §4.4):

\[ c = \mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{posemb}(t)) + v_g, \qquad v_g \in \mathbb R^{1024}. \]

The mathsthe crop, and what a handful of tries can tell you

The crop. A camera with field of view \(\theta\) at distance \(d\) sees a width \(2d\tan(\theta/2)\). To see what a narrower lens \(\theta'\) sees from the same spot, keep the centre fraction

\[ f = \frac{\tan(\theta'/2)}{\tan(\theta/2)} = \frac{\tan(69.4^\circ/2)}{\tan(82.1^\circ/2)} = 0.795 \text{ across}, \qquad \frac{\tan(42.5^\circ/2)}{\tan(52.2^\circ/2)} = 0.794 \text{ down}. \]

Those are spec-sheet angles, not measured ones; the overlay check was by eye.

Six tries. The Wilson interval for \(k\) successes in \(n\) tries, at 95% (\(z = 1.96\)), is

\[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}, \qquad \hat p = k/n . \]

For 6 of 6 the lower end is 0.61; for 0 of 6 the upper end is 0.39; for 96 of 100 the lower end is 0.902, the fewest out of 100 that clear 0.9.

Check yourselfWhy crop the picture instead of moving our camera?

Moving a wider camera further away would fit the same table width, but from a different viewpoint, so the arms and the cloth would look different from their pictures. A crop is what the narrower lens sees from the same spot, so our camera can stay where theirs was.

8The whole of part 1

What happens, end to end, every time the robot asks?

  1. Take the three pictures and the 12 joint angles. Keep the centre 79.4% of the top picture; shrink all three to 224 pixels wide. (chapters 1, 7)
  2. Send them, with the garment guess (none on the first request) and the anchor from the last plan. (4, 6)
  3. SigLIP turns each picture into 256 tokens; Gemma reads them with the 12 joint tokens, the garment token and the query. (5)
  4. The action expert starts 30 moves of noise and nudges them 10 times into a plan, held to the anchor for the first 4. (3, 4)
  5. The server turns the plan's changes back into joint targets and returns the first 5, the next anchor and the model's guesses. (1, 4)
  6. The robot plays the 5 moves at 20 a second. On the first answer it holds still and keeps the garment guess instead. (4, 6)

All of it was learned by copying people (chapter 2), and copying only teaches what the recordings show. In Ilia's training mix his own home recordings, where he corrected the robot as it went, were "the only source of recovery" (report §9.6): the organisers' folds show what to do, not how to get back from a mistake.

Where each part came from

  • 1989Pomerleau: ALVINN, a network that learned to steer a car by copying a driver. Behaviour cloning.
  • 2023Lipman et al.; Liu et al.: flow matching and rectified flow, drawing samples by following a learned velocity from noise.
  • 2023Zhao et al.; Chi et al.: ACT predicts chunks of moves instead of one; Diffusion Policy draws robot moves out of noise.
  • 2024Black et al.: π0, flow matching on top of a vision-language model.
  • 2025Physical Intelligence; Ilia's team: π0.5; the BEHAVIOR-1K solution adds soft inpainting between chunks and correlated noise (report §1.3).
  • 2026Larchenko: LeHome. The garment token and its bootstrap, the prediction heads, per-step normalisation (report §1.3).

Next: folding more often

The checkpoint we run was not trained only by copying. It was fine-tuned on real recordings from a simulation checkpoint that had learned much more (report §9.4, §9.5), and the simulation round is where it learned to fold four of five garments. Part 2 is that story: scoring each moment, the policy judging itself, and practising what worked.

What the checking caught: our recorded videos run at about 11 frames a second while their files say 20, so every time on this page comes from the request log, not the video.
