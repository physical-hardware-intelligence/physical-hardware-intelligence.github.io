Learning to Fold, end to end

How one person trained a vision-language-action policy to fold clothes with two SO-101 arms in Isaac Sim, took first place out of 62 teams, and then moved it to a real robot. Each part is explained from the problem it solves: the maths, the line of code that implements it, and what it did when we ran the released checkpoint ourselves.

Built from the technical report, Ilia Larchenko's released code (lehome_solution at ca73828), his released simulation checkpoint run on our RTX 4080 SUPER on 2026-10-17, the AWR and π*0.6 (RECAP) papers, and Chelsea Finn's CS224R slides (Spring 2026). Written for our reproduction of this work.

Every component gets the same five questions. Why: the problem it solves. How: the mechanism, with maths. Ours: what our own run showed. Fits: where it sits in the loop. Lever: what you could change to do better. Numbers marked derived are computed here from stated values; everything else is read from the report, the code or our logs, with the source named.

In one minute
  1. Start from π0.5, cloned on the organisers' scripted demonstrations: it folds sometimes.
  2. Roll it out in simulation, thousands of times, recording the policy's own estimate of success at every frame.
  3. Turn pass or fail into per-frame credit: checkpoints from the checker's own tests, withdrawn on failure, so every episode still sums to 1 or 0.
  4. Combine that credit with the success and completion estimates into one advantage per frame, falling back on plain outcomes as data ages.
  5. Train on frames drawn in proportion to \(e^{\operatorname{clip}(A,-2,2)}\) (AWR), with a token telling the policy which frames had a non-negative advantage (RECAP).
  6. At test time, keep the token on, push further with guidance, re-plan every 3 to 5 steps, and play the best of 2 or 3 sampled chunks.

0The whole machine

Under the engineering, this is an actor-critic loop with three jobs: collect episodes with the current policy, score every moment of them, and improve the policy toward the moments that scored well. What makes it unusual is how each job is done.

  • The critic is not a separate network. The policy predicts its own chance of success from the camera images, and that prediction is the value function (§4).
  • The policy is never trained with a policy gradient. Its training is supervised, and the advantage enters in two ways. AWR changes how often each frame is drawn for training (§8). RECAP feeds the advantage in as an input the policy can later be asked for (§9).
  • Collection and training never wait for each other. Machines exchange episodes and checkpoints only through the Hugging Face Hub (§12).

One evaluation step, read from the code

Before any learning, here is what happens each time the simulated arms move. This is the inference path we ran ourselves. Training reuses the same loop to collect episodes.

  1. The simulator sends its observation to Ilia's eval worker over HTTP (POST /infer, remote_loop.py). The observation is three 640×480 camera images plus 12 joint angles.
  2. The worker holds the current plan, a chunk of future joint targets from the model server. It hands back the next target. When the executed part of the chunk is used up, it first sends this observation to the model server for a new chunk (eval_worker.py).
  3. The target is 12 numbers, one angle per motor (each arm: five joints and a gripper). The simulator sets them as the joints' position targets (garment_bi_v2.py:281–282).
  4. The physics engine advances by one time step. Each joint's motor is modelled as a spring-damper pulling toward its target; cloth, gravity and contacts move in the same step.
  5. The cameras render, the sensors refresh, and the next observation is read. Joint angles and the top camera are fresh every step; the two wrist cameras refresh every third step.
  6. Every 30th step the competition's checker tests whether the garment is folded; on success the episode stops.
Two clocks

The report describes the simulated action space as 30 Hz, and the organisers' simulation datasets are labelled 30 fps. Inside the simulator, though, each step advances the physics by 1/90 s: the challenge's environment uses a physics step of 1/90 s and one physics step per action (garment_bi_cfg_v2.py: dt = 1/90, decimation = 1). Both are true at once, because the challenge's own recorder writes one dataset frame per simulator step and labels the file 30 fps (dataset_record.py:272).

So "30 frames = 1 s" holds on the data's clock, while the same 30 steps are 0.33 s of simulated physics. Inside the simulator nothing depends on which clock you use, because data, training and evaluation all count in steps. It matters only when sim data is mixed with real data, where the motion per step has to match; §14 shows how Ilia rescales each source's speed.

2Why not just copy, and why not PPO

The starting point is behaviour cloning: train the policy to reproduce the organisers' scripted demonstrations. That gives a policy that sometimes folds. Everything after is about raising that rate.

Why

Cloning clean demonstrations has a known failure. The demonstrations never make mistakes, so they never show how to recover from one. Once the cloned policy drifts into a state the demos never visited, it has no data for that state, and errors compound. Finn's CS224R calls this covariate shift: the states the policy visits stop matching the states it was trained on (lecture 2, slides 23–24). Cloth makes it worse, because a small difference in a grasp leaves the garment in a different shape (report §1.2).

How

A cloned policy that already folds some of the time can improve in two ways (report §2.2):

  1. Fold cleanly on the first try more often. Find the moments where the policy did better than usual and make those its normal behaviour. AWR and RECAP do exactly this.
  2. Learn to recover from a ruined state. That needs examples of recovery, which clean demos lack. DAgger supplies them by having an expert correct the policy in the states it actually visits (lecture 2, slide 25). In simulation, Ilia found teleoperating cloth too hard for this to help much. On the real robot it became one of his most useful tools (§14).
Fits

The simulation round is almost entirely route 1. The real round is route 2.

Why not policy gradients

PPO, and its group-relative variant GRPO, improve a policy by raising the log-probability of actions that did well and lowering it for actions that did badly. That needs \(\log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\), and a flow-matching policy does not give one cheaply: it produces actions by integrating a learned velocity field from noise (§3), and the probability of the result is not tractable. The π*0.6 paper makes the same point about its own flow-matching policy.

Ilia adds a second argument (report §2.2). Valid action chunks occupy a thin set inside the space of all possible 30×12 chunks. A method that pushes probability away from bad actions moves predictions in directions the data never covered, mostly off that set. Reweighting and conditioning only move probability toward actions the policy already produces, so they stay on it. Finn frames the same property for offline RL: methods that supervise only on actions in the data implicitly constrain the policy to the data (lecture 7, slide 28).

Cost

Staying on the data's actions also means almost no exploration. These methods sharpen behaviour the policy already shows; they do not discover new moves. Ilia judged that a good trade for a competition that rewards one reliable fold, and he names the lack of recovery as the main open problem (report §10.4, and §15 here).

Lever

Ilia calls the choice of this family a subjective bet, made without a controlled comparison against PPO-style methods on this task. The π*0.6 paper does compare: on its tasks, an AWR baseline was slower and a PPO baseline stable but poor (its Fig. 11). That comparison was on PI's robots, not this task.

3The policy network

The policy is Ilia's team's BEHAVIOR-1K 2025 model, itself an extension of Physical Intelligence's π0.5, with changes for this task (report §4). Two halves:

  • A prefix that reads the scene. SigLIP-So400m/14 (frozen) turns each 224×224 camera image into tokens, and a Gemma-2B transformer reads them together with the joint state and a few special tokens.
  • An action expert, a Gemma-300M transformer. It reads the prefix's cache and produces a chunk of 30 future moves for all 12 joints.

What the prefix sees, and who sees whom

The prefix's tokens sit in groups under a staircase attention mask: each group sees itself and the groups before it, never the groups after (report §4.1).

Flow matching: how a chunk is made

The action expert does not output actions directly. It learns a velocity field: given a noisy chunk and a noise level, which way to move to get closer to a real chunk. At inference, you start from pure noise and follow that field in 10 steps.

Training, as written in the code (pi_modified.py:1224–1229, 1256). Take a real chunk \(a\) from the data and noise \(\epsilon\). Draw a noise level \(t\) from Beta(1.5, 1), squeezed into (0.001, 1). Mix:

\[x_t = t\,\epsilon + (1-t)\,a, \qquad u_t = \epsilon - a, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \lVert v_\theta(x_t, t, \text{prefix}) - u_t \rVert^2 .\]

Here \(t=1\) is pure noise and \(t=0\) is the clean chunk; \(u_t\) is the constant velocity that carries noise to data in a straight line. Sampling integrates from \(t=1\) down to \(t=0\) with \(S=10\) Euler steps of size \(-0.1\) (pi_modified.py:1471):

\[x_{t-0.1} = x_t - 0.1\, v_\theta(x_t, t, \text{prefix}).\]

Three inherited details shape this. The starting noise is correlated across joints and time, drawn from the data's own action covariance shrunk halfway toward the identity, \(\Sigma_{\text{reg}} = 0.5\,\Sigma + 0.5\,I\). A temperature \(\tau\) scales it by \(\sqrt{\tau}\) (pi_modified.py:1615–1619). Each training example draws 5 independent (noise, \(t\)) pairs that share one prefix pass.

What a chunk contains

A chunk is 30 steps × 12 joints of changes from the current joint state. The training data's targets are converted to deltas, and the server converts the model's output back to absolute targets before anything is sent (training/config.py:416–423, all 12 joints). The delta spread grows with how far ahead a step is, roughly like a random walk, so each future position gets its own normalisation. Ilia fits a smooth curve per joint instead of using noisy per-step statistics (report §4.6):

\[\sigma_d(t) = a_d + s_d\sqrt{t + e_d}.\]

Without it, one global scale would shrink the near steps, the ones actually executed, and let the uncertain far end of the chunk dominate the loss.

4The policy is its own value function

Standard actor-critic training keeps a second network, the critic, to score states. Ilia puts the critic inside the policy: a few cheap linear heads read the same model's representation and predict how the episode is going (report §5).

Why

Training is simpler with one model to train, serve and version. And predicting whether a fold will succeed needs much of the same understanding of the garment as choosing the next move, so the shared representation helps both. All head gradients flow into the backbone; small loss weights keep them from dominating (report §5).

How

Current-frame heads are linear readouts of the single current query token. That token sits in the image group of the attention mask (§3), so it sees pixels only: no joint state, no garment-type input, no advantage. A head that could read the joint state might learn "arm in this pose means success" instead of looking at the cloth.

5The RL you need

Four ideas carry everything in §6 to §10: return, value, advantage, and how to estimate advantage from a noisy value. This is the standard material, in Finn's CS224R notation where it exists, with this task's numbers attached.

Return, value, Q and advantage

The return from step \(t\) is the discounted sum of future rewards, \(G_t = \sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^k r_{t+k}\). The value \(V^\pi(s)\) is the expected return from state \(s\) when acting with policy \(\pi\). \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) is the same expectation after first taking action \(a\). The advantage is the difference (lecture 4, slide 6):

\[A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s), \qquad V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\left[Q^\pi(s,a)\right].\]

Advantage answers the question that matters for improvement: was this action better or worse than what the policy usually does here? It averages to zero over the policy's own actions. Positive means "do more of this", negative means "do less".

Ours

Here the episode's total reward is 1 for a fold and 0 otherwise (§6), so the value of a state is simply the chance of eventually folding from it, minus reward already collected. That is why a success-probability head can serve as the value function.

Why subtract a baseline

Subtracting any quantity that depends only on the state leaves the expected policy-gradient update unchanged, because \(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)\,b(s)] = b(s)\,\nabla\!\int\!\pi = 0\) (lecture 3, slide 17). It can still cut the variance a lot, and the value is the natural choice of baseline. That replaces the noisy return with the advantage (lecture 4, slide 10). In §7, Ilia asks how much of an imperfect value to subtract, and the answer is less than all of it.

One-step, n-step, and GAE

We never know \(Q\) or \(V\) exactly. A learned value \(\hat V\) gives the one-step TD residual:

\[\delta_t = r_t + \gamma\,\hat V(s_{t+1}) - \hat V(s_t).\]

It is low-variance but biased by whatever \(\hat V\) gets wrong. The full Monte Carlo return minus \(\hat V(s_t)\) is unbiased but noisy. Using \(n\) real rewards before bootstrapping sits between the two (lecture 4, slide 20). Generalised advantage estimation (Schulman et al. 2016) averages all the n-step estimates with geometrically decaying weights, which reduces to a short backward recursion:

\[A^{\text{GAE}}_t = \sum_{l\ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l}, \qquad A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,A_{t+1}.\]

\(\lambda = 0\) gives the one-step estimate; \(\lambda = 1\) gives return minus value. Ilia uses \(\gamma = 0.999\) and \(\lambda = 0.99\), so \(\gamma\lambda = 0.98901\): a residual \(l\) steps ahead keeps weight \(0.98901^l\), which halves every 63 steps and falls to about 1/e after 91 derived. One step's advantage therefore mostly reflects what happens over the next few seconds of the data's clock.

Potential-based shaping

Adding \(F_t = \gamma\,\Phi(s_{t+1}) - \Phi(s_t)\) to the reward, for any function \(\Phi\) of state, cannot change which policy is optimal (Ng, Harada and Russell 1999). Summed over an episode the terms telescope, \(\sum_t \gamma^t F_t = \gamma^T\Phi(s_T) - \Phi(s_0)\), so the bonus depends only on where the episode starts and ends. In between, it moves credit earlier: an action that raises \(\Phi\) is rewarded when it happens, not only at the end. Ilia's completion signal is exactly this kind of term, with \(\Phi\) = predicted completion (§7).

Weighted imitation

The last idea is CS224R's bridge from imitation to RL (lecture 7, slides 17–21). Filtered behaviour cloning keeps only good trajectories and clones them. Advantage-weighted imitation clones everything, weighting each sample by \(\exp(A)\):

\[\theta \leftarrow \arg\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{s,a\sim\mathcal D}\big[\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\big(A(s,a)/\beta\big)\big].\]

AWR is the principled version of this line, and §8 derives it. (Finn's slides write the temperature as \(1/\alpha\) and use \(\beta\) for the behaviour policy; this page follows the AWR paper, where \(\beta\) is the temperature.)

6Reward: from pass or fail to checkpoints

Why

The competition only says pass or fail at the end. With that alone, a good grasp at step 40 gets no credit until the very end, and an easy fold looks identical to a hard one (report §6). The fix is to add intermediate credit without changing what the policy is ultimately rewarded for.

How

Ilia builds the intermediate credit from the checker's own distance tests (§1), so nothing new has to be defined. The steps below follow his code (eval/dataset_writer.py: compute_dense_reward).

The credit per checkpoint is one over the number of checkpoints. Shorts are the easiest type, so they get no intermediate checkpoint (report §6.1).

The first checkpoint can be lost: if its tests stop holding, the 0.5 is taken back, and it is paid again if they return. The second checkpoint only ever adds credit. Because the environment refreshes the checker every 30 steps (§1), reward can only change on those frames.

2. Tops get the first half gradually

For tops, the first 0.5 is not paid as one jump when checkpoint 1 is reached. It is paid out as the main "meet" distance, test 1, shrinks. With \(d_t\) that distance ratio, \(m_t = \min_{\tau\le t} d_\tau\) its lowest value so far, and \(t_1\) the frame where checkpoint 1 is first reached:

\[R^{\text{cp1}}_t = 0.5\;\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{d_0 - m_t}{d_0 - d_{t_1}},\,0,\,1\right), \qquad r_t \mathrel{+}= R^{\text{cp1}}_t - R^{\text{cp1}}_{t-1}.\]

Using the running minimum matters: moving the cloth back and forth earns nothing, because only a new closest approach pays. If folding the first sleeve already closes 60% of the gap, it has earned 0.3.

3. On failure, everything is taken back

If the episode fails, every bit of credit it collected is withdrawn, so the episode total is exactly 1 for a fold and 0 otherwise. The withdrawal is spread evenly over the frames after \(t_p\), the last frame at which the running total reached its peak:

\[r_t \mathrel{-}= \frac{\sum_\tau r_\tau}{T - t_p - 1}, \qquad t_p < t < T.\]
Why

Two reasons, both from the report. A checkpoint can mislead: its tests may hold while the rest of the garment is already in a hopeless state, so paying for it would reward a dead end. And taking the credit back in one spike at the last frame would put all the blame on that final step; spreading it out blames the whole stretch after the episode stopped improving.

Effect

On a failed episode that reached the checkpoint, the moves before the checkpoint keep positive credit and the moves after it get negative credit. The advantage (§7) reads this as "the approach was good, what followed was not". Over the whole episode the reward still sums to the true outcome.

What the policy is finally rewarded for

\[\sum_t r_t = \mathbb{1}[\text{success}].\]

Dense reward changes when credit arrives, never how much an episode is worth. Ilia also ignores the simulator's own shaped reward for how neat the final garment looks (report §6).

Lever

Ilia calls reward design partly an art, and his final mix "surely far from optimal" (report §10.1). The checkpoint groups and the 0.5/0.5 split were chosen by hand and never ablated.

7Advantage, term by term

The advantage is the single number per frame that AWR and RECAP consume. Ilia's version has six ingredients. The report calls the final formula over-engineered (report §6.8), so it helps to take it apart in the order the code builds it (eval/dataset_writer.py: compute_gae_advantages).

Intuition

A frame's advantage is high when three things happen after it (report §6.8):

  • the garment makes real progress by the checker's own distances (the reward);
  • the policy's own estimate of folding goes up (the success channel);
  • its estimate of how far through the fold it is goes up (the completion channel).

The older the episode, the less its advantage trusts the model's estimates and the more it falls back on plain outcomes.

Ingredient 1 · the value is the success estimate

Because each episode's reward sums to its outcome (§6), the expected reward still to come from a state is the chance of folding minus what has already been paid (report §6.2):

\[V(s_t) = P(\text{success}\mid s_t) - R^{\text{cum}}_t, \qquad R^{\text{cum}}_t = \sum_{\tau<t} r_\tau .\]

Raw head outputs are noisy, so every consumer uses an exponential moving average, \(\bar S_t = 0.2\,\hat P_t + 0.8\,\bar S_{t-1}\). The head also has a known bias near successful endings (§4). Offline, once the outcome \(y\) is known, the last \(K=30\) smoothed values are replaced by a straight line toward \(y\):

\[\bar S_{T-K+i} = \bar S_{T-K-1} + \big(y - \bar S_{T-K-1}\big)\,\tfrac{i}{K}, \qquad i = 1,\dots,K.\]

In the code below: ema_alpha = 0.2, value_tail_k = \(K\) = 30, success_label = \(y\).

Ingredient 2 · subtract only part of an imperfect value

The textbook move is to subtract the full value from the return (§5). Ilia argues that is only optimal for a perfect value estimate. He borrows CUPED, a control-variate method from A/B testing: the variance-minimising amount to subtract is \(\theta^*\hat V\), where

\[\theta^* = \rho(\hat V, G)\,\frac{\sigma(G)}{\sigma(\hat V)}, \qquad G_t = \mathbb{1}[\text{success}] - R^{\text{cum}}_t .\]

\(\theta^*\) is 1 for a perfect predictor and 0 for a useless one. During the competition he used a fixed \(\alpha_s = 0.5\) instead, believing \(\theta^*\) could not be computed. The report's own correction: it can be estimated from rollouts, and afterwards it measured roughly 0.4 to 0.8 per garment, with 0.5 near the median (report §6.2). So the dampened baseline is

\[\hat V_t = \alpha_s\big(\bar S_t - R^{\text{cum}}_t\big), \qquad \alpha_s = 0.5 .\]

Ingredient 3 · GAE over the success channel

Plug \(\hat V\) into the TD residual and run GAE with \(\gamma = 0.999\), \(\lambda = 0.99\). The terminal value is pinned to the true outcome, \(\bar S_T = y\):

\[\delta^s_t = r_t + \gamma\,\hat V_{t+1} - \hat V_t, \qquad A^s_t = \delta^s_t + \gamma\lambda\,A^s_{t+1}.\]

Expanding \(\hat V\) shows what this does to the reward (report §6.4):

\[\delta^s_t = (1 - \alpha_s\gamma)\,r_t + \alpha_s\big(\gamma\,\bar S_{t+1} - \bar S_t\big) + \alpha_s(1-\gamma)\,R^{\text{cum}}_t \;\approx\; 0.5\,r_t + 0.5\,\big(\bar S_{t+1} - \bar S_t\big).\]

With full subtraction (\(\alpha_s = 1\)), a checkpoint reward would cancel out exactly: it enters the remaining return and \(R^{\text{cum}}\) equally. With \(\alpha_s = 0.5\), half of it survives, so the frame where a checkpoint is reached keeps a positive advantage. The other half of each residual is the change in the success estimate.

Worked

A frame reaches the halfway checkpoint, so \(r_t = 0.5\), and the smoothed success estimate rises from 0.60 to 0.70. With \(\alpha_s = 0.5\):

\[\begin{aligned}\delta^s_t &= (1 - 0.4995)(0.5)\\ &\quad {}+ 0.5\,(0.999\cdot0.70 - 0.60)\\ &= 0.300.\end{aligned}\]

With full subtraction, \(\alpha_s = 1\):

\[\begin{aligned}\delta^s_t &= (0.001)(0.5) + (0.6993 - 0.60)\\ &= 0.100,\end{aligned}\]

and the checkpoint's own reward contributes only 0.0005 of that. The dampening is what keeps the checkpoint moment visibly good derived.

In the code: ema_s_arr = \(\bar S\), cum_before = \(R^{\text{cum}}\), v_hat = \(\hat V\), p_terminal = \(y\), gl = \(\gamma\lambda\).

Ingredient 4 · completion as potential-based shaping

The success estimate saturates above about 0.9 on easy garments and drifts as the policy changes (report §6.3). Completion does neither: what "60% folded" looks like barely depends on which policy did the folding. Ilia adds it as a potential-based term (§5) with \(\Phi_t = \alpha_c\,\bar C_t\), \(\alpha_c = 0.5\), and terminal \(\bar C_T = y\):

\[\delta^c_t = \alpha_c\big(\gamma\,\bar C_{t+1} - \bar C_t\big), \qquad \Phi^c_t = \delta^c_t + \gamma\lambda\,\Phi^c_{t+1}.\]

There is no reward in this channel. It only says whether a frame moved the fold forward.

Ingredient 5 · stale episodes fall back on outcomes

Success estimates are recorded once, at collection time, on states the model had not trained on, and never recomputed: later models have trained on those episodes and would give overconfident answers. But as the policy moves on, old estimates describe an old policy. So each episode's success channel is blended with a segment advantage that uses outcomes only, weighted by how fresh the episode is (report §6.5):

\[\tilde A_t = w\,A^s_t + (1-w)\,A^{\text{seg}}_t + \Phi^c_t, \qquad w = \min(\text{sampling share}, 1).\]

An episode's sampling share starts at 1.0 and is multiplied by 0.98 each time a new batch of rollouts arrives; once it would fall below 0.1, the dataset is dropped (§12). The completion term stays at full strength for every episode, because completion does not go stale.

The segment advantage splits an episode at its first checkpoint and compares each part's reward with what is typical for that garment. Here \(p_{\text{cp}}\) is the garment's checkpoint rate, SR its success rate, and \(G(n) = (1-(\gamma\lambda)^n)/(1-\gamma\lambda)\) rescales a flat per-step value to the size a GAE sum would have:

\[A^{\text{seg}}_t = \begin{cases}\big(R_1 - \tfrac12 p_{\text{cp}}\big)\,G(n_1)/n_1 & t \le t_1\\[3pt] \big(R_2 - (\text{SR}/p_{\text{cp}} - \tfrac12)\big)\,G(n_2)/n_2 & t > t_1\end{cases}\]

An episode that never reaches the checkpoint gets \((R - \text{SR})\,G(T)/T\) on every frame. Reaching the checkpoint and then failing gives a positive advantage before the checkpoint and a negative one after, the same shape as the reward (§6).

Ingredient 6 · one scale, one clip, one bonus

The blended advantage is divided by a single standard deviation \(\sigma\), computed over unbiased rollouts whose sampling share is still at least 0.5, and clipped to \([-2, 2]\). The config's beta multiplies \(\sigma\) first; it is 1.0. Then the precision boost (report §6.6): per garment, the top 20% of successful episodes, ranked by how tightly the final frame passed its tightest test, get \(+0.3\) on every frame. Garments with fewer than 5 successes in the window are skipped. That nudges training toward clean folds over borderline ones.

The margin for a "meet" test is \(1 - d^{(i)}\) and for a "stay apart" test \(d^{(i)} - 1\); the score of an episode is its smallest margin.

What the training run actually used

Every value above is set in the released sim pipeline config. In the first iteration (mode: auto) only segment advantages exist, since there are no recorded estimates yet; GAE is used from the second iteration on.

Code over report

Two things the code adds to the report. The organisers' demonstrations and DAgger episodes get a fixed advantage of 0.3 for the RECAP input (lines 111 and 119), and demonstrations are masked out of the success-head loss. How often those frames are drawn is set separately, by garment failure rate (§8). The report's per-frame formulas apply to RL rollouts only.

8AWR, to the core

Advantage-weighted regression (Peng, Kumar, Zhang and Levine, 2019) turns "do more of what worked" into plain supervised learning. This section derives it from the AWR paper, shows how Finn's course frames it, and then shows the one change Ilia makes and the code that implements it.

The problem AWR solves

You have data from some behaviour policy \(\mu\). Here \(\mu\) is the mix of the organisers' demos and every past checkpoint's rollouts. You want a policy \(\pi\) that does better than \(\mu\). The exact gain of switching from \(\mu\) to \(\pi\) is the advantage of \(\mu\) averaged over the states and actions \(\pi\) would visit (AWR Eq. 3):

\[J(\pi) - J(\mu) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_\pi}\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\big[A^\mu(s,a)\big].\]

We don't have states from \(\pi\), only from \(\mu\). So AWR swaps in \(\mu\)'s states, which is accurate only while \(\pi\) stays close to \(\mu\). Closeness becomes a constraint (Eqs. 4–6):

\[\max_\pi\; \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_\mu}\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}\big[A^\mu(s,a)\big] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_\mu}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\,\big\|\,\mu(\cdot\mid s)\big) \le \epsilon .\]

The closed-form answer

Turn the constraint into a penalty with weight \(\beta\), and require \(\pi\) to integrate to 1. Then, state by state, maximise over the distribution \(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\) (Eq. 7 and Appendix A):

\[\int \pi(a\mid s)\,A(s,a)\,da \;-\; \beta\int \pi(a\mid s)\log\frac{\pi(a\mid s)}{\mu(a\mid s)}\,da \;+\; \alpha_s\Big(1 - \int \pi(a\mid s)\,da\Big).\]

Setting the derivative with respect to each \(\pi(a\mid s)\) to zero gives \(A(s,a) - \beta\big(\log\pi - \log\mu + 1\big) - \alpha_s = 0\), so \(\log\pi = \log\mu + A/\beta + \text{const}\):

\[\pi^*(a\mid s) = \frac{1}{Z(s)}\,\mu(a\mid s)\,\exp\!\big(A(s,a)/\beta\big).\]

This is AWR Eq. 8, and it is the whole idea in one line. Take the data's own action distribution and tilt it: actions better than average gain probability in proportion to \(e^{A/\beta}\), worse ones lose it. The temperature \(\beta\) sets how far you may move. Large \(\beta\) stays near \(\mu\); small \(\beta\) piles almost everything onto the best actions seen.

From the ideal policy to a training loss

The ideal \(\pi^*\) cannot be written down for a neural network. So AWR finds the network closest to it, minimising \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi^*\,\|\,\pi_\theta)\). Because \(\pi^*\) is \(\mu\) times a weight, the expectation over \(\pi^*\) becomes an expectation over the data with that weight attached (Eqs. 9–10):

\[\theta \leftarrow \arg\max_\theta\; \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\;\exp\!\big(A(s,a)/\beta\big)\Big].\]

That is behaviour cloning with a weight on every sample. The paper drops the per-state normaliser \(Z(s)\) in this practical form. Two KL directions appear: the constraint keeps \(\pi\) near \(\mu\) with \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\mu)\), while the projection fits the network with \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi^*\|\pi_\theta)\).

Limits

Two ends of \(\beta\), derived here from Eq. 8 (the paper does not discuss them) derived. As \(\beta\to\infty\), every weight tends to 1 and AWR becomes plain behaviour cloning of the data. As \(\beta\to 0\), the weights concentrate on the highest-advantage samples, which is close to filtered behaviour cloning, the simple baseline CS224R recommends comparing against (lecture 7, slide 17).

As run

The AWR paper fits a separate value network by regression on TD(\(\lambda\)) returns with \(\lambda = 0.95\). It uses a fixed \(\beta = 0.05\) and clips weights at \(\omega_{\max} = 20\). Its replay buffer is first-in, first-out, holding the latest 50k samples. Each iteration it collects about 2,000 samples, takes 200 value steps and 1,000 policy steps, with minibatches of 256 (Appendix C). With replay, \(\mu\) is a mixture of past policies, and one shared value network serves as the baseline for all of them (Eqs. 40–41, 16).

CS224R

Finn presents AWR as the principled form of weighted imitation (lecture 7, slides 18–21). Its strength is that it never trains on, or queries, an action outside the data. Its weakness is that a Monte Carlo value estimate is noisy.

Ilia's version: weight the sampler, not the loss

Ilia keeps AWR's target and changes where the weight is applied (report §2.3). Instead of loading every frame and multiplying its loss by \(e^{A/\beta}\), he draws frames in proportion to their weight and leaves the loss unweighted:

\[P(\text{draw frame } i) \;\propto\; e^{\operatorname{clip}(A_i,\,-2,\,2)}, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \text{plain flow-matching loss on the drawn batch}.\]

The two are equal in expectation. If \(w_i\) is frame \(i\)'s weight and \(p_i = w_i/\sum_j w_j\), then

\[\mathbb{E}_{i\sim p}\big[\nabla\ell_i\big] = \sum_i p_i\,\nabla\ell_i = \frac{1}{\sum_j w_j}\sum_i w_i\,\nabla\ell_i ,\]

which is the weighted-loss gradient times a positive constant. The difference is cost. A frame with weight \(e^{-2}\) is almost never drawn, so its three camera images are never decoded and it never takes a batch slot. Compute goes where the weight is.

Worked

Three frames with advantages \(-1\), \(0\) and \(1.5\) get weights \(e^{-1} = 0.37\), \(1\) and \(e^{1.5} = 4.48\). So they are drawn with probabilities 6.3%, 17.1% and 76.6%. Their importance weights for the heads, \(1/(Np_i)\) with \(N = 3\), are 5.30, 1.95 and 0.44. The rarely drawn frame counts five times when it does appear, so the heads still see each frame equally on average derived.

Temperature

The advantage arriving here has already been divided by its global standard deviation \(\sigma\) (§7). So, in raw units, Ilia's sampler is AWR with temperature \(\beta = \sigma\) derived. The config's beta: 1.0 multiplies \(\sigma\), so it scales this temperature.

Clipping

AWR clips the weight from above at 20 and lets it fall toward zero. Ilia clips the advantage at \(\pm 2\), which bounds the weight on both sides: from \(e^{-2} = 0.135\) to \(e^{2} = 7.39\). The best frame is drawn at most 54.6 times as often as the worst derived. The code logs this ratio for every dataset it loads.

Keeping the heads honest

Sampling by advantage tilts the data, which is what the action loss should see. The value heads must not see it that way: the success head has to learn the true chance of success, not the chance among frames AWR liked. So every drawn frame carries an importance weight that undoes the tilt, and the auxiliary losses use it while the action loss ignores it (report §2.3):

\[w_i = \frac{1}{N\,p_i\,T_{\text{ep}(i)}} .\]

\(1/(Np_i)\) cancels the sampling probability. Dividing by the episode's length \(T_{\text{ep}}\) also makes each episode count once in the head losses, however long it ran.

The other sources

Not every frame has an advantage. Frames from the organisers' demonstrations and from DAgger are drawn by how hard their garment still is, \(P \propto e^{3(1-\text{SR}_{\text{garment}})}\), with the same importance weights for the heads (report §2.3). Old rollout datasets fade: each dataset's share of the batch is multiplied by 0.98 whenever new rollouts arrive, and drops out below 0.1. In AWR's terms, that makes \(\mu\) a recency-weighted mixture of past policies.

Fits

AWR shapes which frames the policy studies. It cannot tell the policy, at test time, to act like its best frames; it only makes those frames more common in training. That second job is RECAP's (§9).

Lever

The clip range \(\pm2\), the 0.98 decay and \(\beta = 1\) were never ablated. They set how hard the policy is pushed toward its best frames and how fast old behaviour is forgotten.

9RECAP, to the core

RECAP comes from Physical Intelligence's π*0.6 paper (2025): RL with experience and corrections via advantage-conditioned policies. AWR changes how often the policy sees each moment. RECAP instead tells the policy which moments were good, as an input, and at test time asks for good.

The idea in one equation

Label every training frame with an indicator \(I\) meaning "this action was better than usual". Train one network on frames both with the label (\(\pi(a\mid o, I)\)) and without it (\(\pi(a\mid o)\)). By Bayes' rule, the labelled policy is the unlabelled one tilted by how likely the label is for each action:

\[\pi(a\mid o, I) = \pi(a\mid o)\,\frac{p(I\mid o, a)}{p(I\mid o)} .\]

If the label depends only on the advantage, \(p(I\mid o,a) = p(I\mid A(o,a))\), this is the same shape as AWR's \(\mu(a\mid s)\,e^{A/\beta}\): the data's actions, reweighted by a rising function of their advantage. The difference is where the tilt lives. AWR applies a fixed curve \(e^{A/\beta}\) to the data. RECAP lets the network learn the tilt from the labels, and the tilt can be switched on or off at inference.

Why it improves the policy

π*0.6 rests on a result from classifier-free-guidance RL (Frans et al. 2025). Sample from

\[\hat\pi(a\mid o) \;\propto\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid o)\;p\big(I\mid A^{\pi_{\text{ref}}}(o,a)\big)^{\beta} .\]

If \(p(I\mid A)\) increases with \(A\), then \(\hat\pi\) is at least as good as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): \(J(\hat\pi)\ge J(\pi_{\text{ref}})\) (π*0.6 §III). With \(\beta = 1\) this is just "condition on \(I\)". With \(\beta > 1\) the tilt is sharpened, and that is classifier-free guidance, written on velocities in §11.

Ilia's version, as the code does it

Ilia keeps the idea and changes almost every detail (report §4.3).

  • No threshold. The advantage token is masked (neutral) whenever \(A < 0\). When \(A\ge0\), it is masked at random, less often the larger \(A\) is: the masking probability falls linearly from 0.5 at \(A = 0\) to 0.1 at \(A\ge2\).

  • Stronger guidance. At inference the token is always on, and guidance runs at 7 to 9.

Read as a label, the gate gives a token that is present with probability

\[P(\text{token on}\mid A) = \begin{cases}0 & A < 0\\[2pt] 0.5 + 0.4\,\min(A/2,\,1) & A\ge 0\end{cases}\]

derived from lines 363–370. At \(A = -0.5\) the token is never on; at \(A = 0\), half the time; at \(A = 1\), 70%; at \(A \ge 2\), 90%. Why

Conditioning uses every frame. A negative-advantage frame still trains the unconditional model, which is what guidance subtracts against, so nothing is thrown away. AWR alone would mostly ignore such frames. And only conditioning gives a knob at test time, the guidance scale, to ask for better than the average good behaviour.

Ours

Lever

The ramp from 0.5 to 0.1 and its saturation at \(A = 2\) were set by hand. π*0.6 binarises at a percentile instead. Which labelling works better for this task is untested.

10AWR and RECAP together

Ilia uses both at once, because they act at different points. AWR acts on the training data: good frames are drawn more often. RECAP acts on the question asked at test time: produce what your positive-advantage frames did. Guidance then pushes further in that direction. The report's Figure 3 makes the point with a toy; the explorer below is that toy, computed exactly.

Toy

One action dimension. The data mixes a large "bad" mode (70%) and a smaller "good" mode (30%). Each action's advantage is a smooth function, negative around the bad mode and positive around the good one. Advantage estimates are noisy, and the noise slider sets how noisy. Four distributions are drawn:

  • data: what the rollouts contain;
  • AWR: the data reweighted by \(e^{\operatorname{clip}(A,-2,2)}\), what training sees;
  • + RECAP: that, times the probability the token is on (Ilia's gate, averaged over the estimate noise), which is what conditioning on the token asks for;
  • + guidance: the gate raised to the guidance scale, the sharpened version.
Read

The readout gives the share of each distribution on the good mode. With noise, some bad actions get a positive estimate and the token, so RECAP alone leaves some "spillover", as the report's figure shows. Doing both, and adding guidance, leaves almost nothing on the bad mode.

The toy multiplies densities exactly. A flow-matching policy only approximates this: guidance extrapolates velocities (§11), which matches sampling from the sharpened density only in the limit of a perfect score model. At high scales, real samples can also drift off the action manifold.

11Inference: guidance, chunks, best-of-N

The same weights fold more or less often depending on how they are run (report §7). Nothing here changes the weights. The model server is stateless: each request is one observation in, one chunk out. Anything that looks like memory, such as the chunk being played, the anchor for the next chunk or the latched garment type, lives in the client (the eval worker in sim, the robot program on hardware).

Making one chunk: 10 Euler steps, each with guidance

Every chunk is 30 steps × 12 joints, made by integrating the learned velocity from noise (\(t = 1\)) to clean actions (\(t = 0\)) in \(S = 10\) steps (§3). At each step the action expert runs twice over the same prefix cache: once with the advantage signal on (in both the token and the AdaRMS vector), once with it off. The two velocities are combined:

\[\hat v = v_{\text{uncond}} + s\,\big(v_{\text{cond}} - v_{\text{uncond}}\big).\]

With \(s = 1\) this is plain conditioning; \(s > 1\) extrapolates past it, in the direction that separates "good" from "any" behaviour. In score terms, \(v_{\text{cond}} - v_{\text{uncond}}\) plays the role of \(\nabla_a \log p(I\mid a)\), and \(s\) is the exponent \(\beta\) of §9. The expensive vision-language pass runs once per chunk; guidance only doubles the cheap action expert.

Soft inpainting keeps consecutive chunks consistent. Without it, each new plan could jump to a different way of doing the fold. The anchor's targets gently pull the new chunk's matching steps toward them during the early, noisy part of denoising. The pull spreads to the rest of the chunk through the action covariance, then switches off for the final steps so the chunk can still correct itself (report §7.2). The bandit preferred light anchoring: some beats none, strong anchoring hurts.

Short execution windows won. The bandit converged to 3 to 5 executed steps out of 30 predicted, which suggests the model's near-term predictions are far more reliable than its distant ones (report §7.7).

Best-of-N, and how often it gives up

With \(N > 1\), the server denoises \(N\) chunks from the same prefix with different seed noise. It scores each by the action-conditioned success residual \(\Delta_{\text{success}}\) (§4), averaged over the conditional and unconditional passes, and plays the best. If every candidate is predicted to make things worse (all scores negative), it draws \(2N\) more and picks from the pooled \(3N\) (report §7.4).

Ours

In our 16-episode smoke test, 680 of 1,241 plans (55%) triggered the redraw. By type: long pants 54%, shorts 67%, long tops 55%, short tops 48%. Even after redrawing, the chosen chunk's predicted gain was positive only 34% to 53% of the time, with a median near zero.

That fits two statements in the report. The residual head's predictions barely correlated with real outcomes in Ilia's experiments. And best-of-N seems to help mainly by avoiding the occasional bad chunk at rare decision points, not by finding a best one (report §7.4). It also means the average plan costs about twice the action-expert passes the table suggests derived.

Which garment is this?

The policy takes the garment type as an input, but at test time nobody says what it is. The client works it out (report §7.6):

  1. A throwaway first call reads the model's own garment-type prediction from the first frame.
  2. The first few plans refine it by majority vote, and these plans also run with a short window of 5 executed steps for every type, so the policy reacts quickly while the type settles.
  3. The type is then frozen and fed back as the input for the rest of the episode.

In our runs the first guess was right on all 16 episodes and never changed: 0 for long tops, 1 for short tops, 2 for long pants, 3 for shorts.

Tuning seven knobs with a bandit

Every knob in the table was tuned per garment type during rollout collection, not by grid search (report §7.7). Each candidate value of each knob is an arm with a Beta posterior, and the knobs are treated independently. For an exploration episode, each knob draws one sample per arm and takes the largest (Thompson sampling). After the episode, its outcome minus the garment type's average success rate updates the chosen arms. Posteriors decay toward uniform each iteration (factor 0.90 in the config) so the bandit follows the moving policy; replay and curriculum episodes are excluded because they are deliberately easier or harder. Once settled, the best values were frozen for the submission.

12The flywheel: collecting data

Simulation speed limits everything: the report puts one episode at about 30 s in Isaac Sim, without saying on what hardware or for how many steps (report §3). Ours took 1.3 to 5.8 minutes each on our RTX 4080 SUPER (§13). So the collection side exists to get as many useful episodes as possible out of each sim-hour.

The plumbing

  • Parallel sims, one server. Each machine runs 3–5 simulator processes, because the competition environment holds one scene per process. Each is a thin client of one shared, stateless policy server; chunks are cached on the client.
  • Stop early. An episode stops on success, and a stuck detector ends hopeless ones. A watchdog restarts simulators that hang.
  • Record everything at collection time. Every episode is saved with the success and completion estimates the policy made while acting. Those are what §7 consumes, never re-predicted later. Each episode also gets a debug video with live reward, value and advantage traces.

The stuck detector, as the code defines it: over the last 120 frames (4 s on the data's clock, 1.3 s of physics), the joint states, the actions and the success estimate must all have a mean standard deviation below 0.01. The episode is then cut back to keep only the first 30 frames of the stuck stretch.

Saving states worth replaying

Replays need saved physics states: every cloth particle's position and velocity, plus the joints.

  • Success states are saved at step 5 of episodes that end in a fold, with probability \(1 - \text{SR}\), so rare successes are always kept.
  • Failure states are saved at the moment the policy visibly ruins a promising episode. The rule: the smoothed success estimate drops more than 0.12 below its running maximum, after that maximum has passed 0.25, ignoring the first 3 plans.
  • Semi-success states come from failed episodes that reached the first checkpoint or held a high estimate. They can also go to the human DAgger queue.

Augmenting the simulator

Augmentation is applied at two scopes (report §3.3). Per episode: garment texture swaps, colour remaps, small changes to the garment's pose, scale and roughness, camera position and focal length, robot base position, table texture and light direction. Per step: garment tint, arm colour and light colour. Normal rollouts used a light regime, enough variety without hurting the live policy: texture swap and colour remap with probability 0.4, ±2 cm and ±2° of garment pose, ±5% scale. Success replays used much stronger settings, since the policy's performance there does not matter.

The training loop

  1. Download whatever new rollouts have arrived.
  2. Recompute advantages over every rollout dataset (§7).
  3. Train about 1,000 steps, drawing frames from all sources at once (§8).
  4. Upload a checkpoint every 500 steps.

Datasets are never merged; the mix is a setting. Each new rollout dataset enters at share 1.0 and fades by 0.98 per update. The demonstrations keep a fixed share (no decay in the config), and a few old successful datasets were kept at a higher weight for the hardest garments (report §2.4).

Rollbacks. Three times in the main round, and once in the second, Ilia went back to a checkpoint from a few days earlier and retrained it on everything collected since. The rolled-back model sees a large, varied, partly off-policy batch at once, and this reliably got training out of local optima (report §2.5). π*0.6 does the same thing systematically, restarting every round from the pre-trained checkpoint.

14The real-robot round

The final round, at ICRA 2026, ran the top 8 teams' policies on a physical robot. Ilia had about three months for simulation and a little over one week for this (report §9). It was second place, with 865 of 1,080 points, behind 895.

Why the sim policy did not transfer

Zero-shot transfer failed, and Ilia's evidence points to overfitting on rendering details. His model resizes each 640×480 image to 224×224. Resizing in two steps instead, to 320×240 first and then to 224×224, is a change people can barely see, yet it cut the simulation success rate significantly. The model's heads could tell the two resizings apart perfectly (report §9.1). The real data also folded some garments differently from the simulation, and Ilia never had the evaluation robot itself: the transfer was sim, to his robot, to theirs.

The recipe

  1. Start from a strong but not the latest sim checkpoint, the latest being the most specialised to the simulator.
  2. Strip the sim-only machinery. Still trained: the action loss, the garment-type head and the completion head. Loaded but frozen: success, checkpoint and time to completion. Removed: the keypoint head, both future-prediction heads, the advantage token and AdaRMS vector, and therefore guidance and best-of-N. Real inference is the plain path: one denoised chunk, soft inpainting and the garment-type bootstrap.
  3. Fine-tune on three buckets of data (table below).
  4. Align the rigs: a camera-overlay tool drives the robot to a dataset frame's joint state and overlays the live cameras on that frame.
  5. Augment heavily: per-camera colour, gain and gamma, blur, noise, crop, rotation and zoom, cutout, camera dropout, and noise or dropout on the joint state so the policy trusts images over a miscalibrated arm.
  6. Match motion speed across sources (below).
  7. Collect DAgger data and weight it by closeness to an intervention (below).

Speed factors: matching motion per step

The model predicts joint changes over a fixed number of steps. A slow source teaches small changes per step and a fast one large changes, so mixing them naively teaches an inconsistent idea of how far to move in one step. Each source's time axis is resampled before training: organiser data ×1.0, home teleop ×1.5, DAgger corrections ×2.0, sim rollouts ×0.65 (report §9.9). A factor above 1 compresses a slow source; below 1 stretches a fast one. The RL-trained sim policy was the fastest of all.

DAgger weighting without a value function

On the real robot there is no checker, so no reward, value or advantage. Ilia used the interventions themselves as the signal (report §9.10):

  • frames where the human was driving get the highest sampling weight;
  • autonomous frames far from any intervention get a low weight;
  • autonomous frames in the 5 s before a takeover ramp down to zero, so the moves that caused the failure are not reinforced.

He calls this a deliberately crude stand-in for a real advantage function. A learned value on the real robot would bring back conditioning, guidance and best-of-N.

The units bug

Between two LeRobot 0.4.x releases, the SO-101's default joint representation changed from a −100…100 range-of-motion scale to degrees. Across a roughly 200° range the two look almost the same, yet the mismatch skews every joint by about 10%. Ilia found it two days before the deadline and fixed it by post-processing (report §9.11).

15What is still open

Ilia names two problems himself (report §10.4–10.5).

Recovery

Every tool in the simulation round pushes toward behaviour already in the data. Conditioning and guidance amplify the average good behaviour, best-of-N picks the least bad of similar samples, and inpainting keeps the policy in the mode it chose. None of them invents a recovery move. His attempts at exploration mostly pushed chunks off the action manifold. The only reliable source of recovery was a human, and that only worked on the real robot.

One pipeline

Round one had the full RL machinery but no recovery data. Round two had recovery data but ran as plain behaviour cloning, because there was no real-side reward or value. A single pipeline would combine them: a real-side value function driving conditioning and best-of-N, prioritised sampling over real rollouts, and DAgger weighted by that same value. Ilia guesses 90% or better is reachable on this task.

17Misreadings to avoid

  1. "AWR weights the loss." Not here. The loss is unweighted; the weight decides how often a frame is drawn (§8). Only the value heads use a weight, and that one undoes the tilt.
  2. "The advantage token is a binary threshold, as in π*0.6." It is a random gate: never on for negative advantage, on 50% to 90% of the time for non-negative advantage, more often the higher the advantage (§9).
  3. "Dense reward changes what the policy is rewarded for." Every episode's reward still sums to exactly 1 for a fold and 0 otherwise. The checkpoints only move credit earlier (§6).
  4. "The success head is a separate critic." It is a linear readout of the policy's own representation, from pixels only (§4).
  5. "Best-of-N finds the best chunk." In our run, 55% of plans found every candidate predicted to be worse than expected, and the chosen score was near zero on median. Its value is mostly in avoiding bad chunks (§11).
  6. "30 steps is one second." On the data's clock, yes. In the simulator's physics, 30 steps are a third of a second (§0).
  7. "The episode stops the moment the garment is folded." The checker runs every 30 steps in the code we ran, so the fold is noticed up to 29 steps late (§1).
  8. "Same seed, same result." Our repeat of the same 16 episodes did not reproduce every outcome (§13).
  9. "The real-robot policy uses all of this." The real round dropped advantage conditioning, guidance and best-of-N. It was behaviour cloning on mixed data, with intervention-weighted sampling (§14).
