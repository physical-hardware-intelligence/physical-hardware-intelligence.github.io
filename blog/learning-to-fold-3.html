The competition assumed garments start out flat (report §9.10), and the organisers' recordings are 60% of what it trained on. A towel opened out wide, or turned and shifted, is a start it has probably seen less of. Two runs can't prove that's the reason, but it is the one difference we noted.

2Nine in a row is not always

The towel folded nine times out of nine on video. Does it always fold?

No count can tell you that. A robot that folds 80% of the time still folds nine in a row about one time in eight (0.89 = ). So the honest answer is a range: every success rate that wouldn't be surprised by what we saw.

There is a rule of thumb for the two cases that come up most:

The rule of four. After n successes in a row, the true rate could be as low as about n / (n + 4). After n failures in a row, it could be as high as about 4 / (n + 4).

Nine towel folds in nine: at least 9/13, about 70%. Ten shorts failures in ten: at most 4/14, about 28%. (We count the nine on video; a tenth fold was seen by eye only.) Try your own numbers:

To say "it folds towels more than 90% of the time", you need 35 folds in a row, or 96 out of 100. That's why chapter 8 asks for every run to be written down, failures included: a page of notes is worth more than a good video.

The mathsthe Wilson interval, and where the four comes from

With k successes in n tries, \(\hat p = k/n\), and \(z = 1.96\) for 95%, the Wilson interval is

\[ \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \;\pm\; z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}}. \]

It holds every rate \(p\) for which \(\hat p\) lies within \(z\) standard errors of \(p\). At \(k = n\), \(\hat p = 1\) and the square root is \(z/2n\), so the lower end is

\[ \frac{1 + \frac{z^2}{2n} - \frac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} = \frac{n}{n + z^2}, \qquad z^2 = 3.84 \approx 4. \]

At \(k = 0\) the same steps give an upper end of \(z^2/(n + z^2)\). Those are the rule of four. For a floor above 0.9 in a row, \(n/(n+3.84) > 0.9\), so \(n > 34.6\): 35.

The codethe interval, as this page's checks compute it
def wilson(k, n, z=1.959964):
    p, d = k / n, 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

Check yourselfThe white shorts failed once. What does that tell you about white shorts?

Almost nothing. Zero in one allows any rate up to 4/5 by the rule of four (79% exactly). One run is a story, not a measurement.

3Why the shorts don't fold

Ten tries, no fold. What went wrong?

We don't know yet. But the logs rule some things out, and that narrows the search.

Not only the garment guess. Part 1, chapter 6 showed that the policy guesses the garment from its first look and keeps that answer. On our shorts it often guessed wrong: long pants or a long top. But in the five runs where it worked with "shorts", guessed by itself in three and set by us in two, none folded either.

It never thought it was close. Its progress head never passed on any shorts run, against at least 0.93 on every towel fold.

Folds that came undone. Twice a first fold happened and was lost: adult boxers on 29 October folded once by about 30 seconds, then the right arm pulled it open; one of the kid-shorts runs reached a fold and lost it.

One run looked broken. In the first kid-shorts run, with the label set to shorts from the start, the left gripper never opened past out of 100. The right arm worked alone and closed its gripper 6 times.

Size, by eye. In the top camera the adult boxers look about one and a half times the size of the organisers' kids' shorts, and run off the top of the picture. The kid shorts are closer in size and failed too.

What would tell us more is a person stepping in at the moment it goes wrong and showing the right move. That is also exactly the data that teaches it (chapter 5).

Check yourselfWhy isn't fixing the garment guess enough?

Because the five runs that already had the right label folded nothing. A fix for the guess can't be judged on shorts until something else changes too.

4Better runs, same weights

Before training anything: what can we change in how we run it?

Park when it's done

In the third towel run on 30 October, the towel was folded by about 50 seconds. At seconds the right gripper closed on it again and began pulling it open. Nothing in our loop tells the policy to stop; our robot program only stops on a clock, here at 75 seconds.

Chapter 1 showed that the progress head knows when a fold is done. So: park the arms once it has said 0.9 or more on every answer for 2 seconds. Replayed on all 21 logs, that rule would have parked 9 of the 10 folds, and none of the 11 failures. Pick a run:

The one fold it misses is the first towel run on 29 October: progress touched 0.94 and dipped back within 2 seconds, and the arms went back to rest by themselves.

We picked 0.9 and 2 seconds after looking at these same runs, so this is a fit, not a test. The rule isn't built yet; the next runs we make are its test.

Set the garment for a fair test

The policy reads its own first guess (part 1, chapter 6), and our robot program can set the label instead. On the towel it makes little difference: one run guessed shorts at first and still folded. On shorts, chapter 3 showed the right label isn't enough. Setting it is still worth doing in a test, so one wrong first look doesn't decide the run.

Stop waiting between plans

Part 1, chapter 4: the policy was trained at 20 moves a second, and our loop plays about . For each plan it asks for, the arms wait about s for the answer, then play 5 moves in 0.25 s. If we asked for the next plan while the current one plays, the answer would arrive before the moves ran out, and the arms would never wait. Not built yet either: the new plan would have to start from where the arm will be, not where it is, and the anchor of part 1, chapter 4, which joins a new plan onto the old one, is the piece that would do it.

The mathsthe park rule, and the timing

With \(c_i\) the progress at answer \(i\), received at time \(t_i\), the rule parks at the first time \(t\) for which

\[ c_i \ge 0.9 \quad \text{for every answer } i \text{ with } t - 2\,\mathrm{s} \le t_i \le t. \]

For the waiting: the time between answers is about 0.46 s in our logs, of which 5 moves at 20 a second take 0.25 s. The rest, about 0.21 s, is waiting. Asking early works when the wait is shorter than the playing, \(0.21 < 0.25\).

The codethe rule, replayed on a log
def park_time(rows, thr=0.9, hold=2.0):
    t = [r["t"] - rows[0]["t"] for r in rows]
    c = [r["completion_pred"] for r in rows]
    for i in range(len(rows)):
        j = i
        while j < len(rows) and c[j] >= thr:
            j += 1
        if j > i and t[j - 1] - t[i] >= hold:
            return t[i] + hold
    return None

runs.py for this page, trimmed. completion_pred is the progress head's answer in each line of a run's calls.jsonl.

Check yourselfThe rule never parked a failure in 11. Does that mean it never will?

No. By the rule of four, zero in 11 still allows a false park in up to 4/15 of failures (26% exactly). Only more runs can bring that down.

5Showing it the right move

To fold our shorts it needs examples of folding our shorts. Where do they come from?

Recording a person folding from start to finish is slow, and it shows only clean folds. Ilia did something faster on his real robot: the policy folds, a person watches, and the moment it starts to go wrong the person takes over with the leader arms, corrects it, and hands back (report §9.10). This is DAgger (part 2, chapter 10). He called those few seconds of correction "one of the most useful tools in the project" (report §10.4).

Our robot program is his recording script, modified, so every run we make is already a recording in his format, with a column that says who drove each frame. So far it says the policy, on all 12,234 frames of our 19 recordings. Nobody can take over yet: we run with the leader arms limp, for safety.

Training doesn't treat every frame of such a recording the same. A frame a person drove counts 2.0. A frame the policy drove counts 0.3. And the policy's frames in the 5 seconds before a takeover fade to 0, because those are the moves that led into trouble. Imagine a takeover in one of our kid-shorts runs:

A short correction ends up as a large share of what training learns from that recording. And the other side: a recording where nobody steps in is the policy copying itself, mistakes and all. Our ten shorts runs as they are would teach it to fail at shorts. Only corrections and clean folds should go into training.

To take over, the leader arms have to follow the followers while the policy drives, so a person can grab them mid-motion without a jump (report §9.10). Ilia's script does that; we switched it off. Switching it back on moves the arms on their own, so it comes back the careful way: a dry run first, one arm at a time, with a person watching.

The mathsthe weight of each frame, and slow corrections

With \(d_t\) the time from frame \(t\) to the next frame a person drove (infinite if there is none),

\[ w_t = \begin{cases} 2.0 & \text{a person drove frame } t \\ 0.3\,\min\!\big(1,\, d_t / 5\,\mathrm s\big) & \text{the policy drove it.} \end{cases} \]

Training draws frame \(t\) with probability \(w_t / \sum_s w_s\). Corrections are slow, with episodes about twice as long as the organisers' (report §9.9), so a plan cut from a person's frames is first sped up by a factor of 2: its 30 moves are taken from 60 recorded frames. Our robot program writes one frame per move the arms make (486 frames for the 490 moves of part 1's run 4), so the policy's own frames are already at the model's pace. Ilia's example sets 2.0 for those too (L188); we don't know why, and would check before copying it.

6Fine-tuning the weights

With corrections recorded, how do the weights change?

The checkpoint we downloaded is the weights alone: 10.5 GB, and no optimizer state. That is enough to fine-tune. Training starts a fresh optimizer from those weights, as Ilia did when he moved from the simulator to the real robot: his file starts from the simulation weights (L29). For us the start would be the real weights instead.

His recipe mixes three kinds of recording in every training batch:

Report §9.6, table 6; shares in train_real_bc.yaml L128–130. Our corrections would go in the 30% bucket. His home recordings aren't in the checkpoint; ours would take their place. The organiser recordings keep the policy anchored to the folds it already does.

A trap in the file. His learning rate warms up over 100,000 steps (L32), in a run of 50,000 (L45). So that run ends halfway up the warm-up, at half the peak rate. Copy the file into a short fine-tune of 2,000 steps and the rate only reaches 2% of its peak: the weights would barely move. Set the warm-up for the run you mean to do.

The file saves every 500 steps (L49) and can resume (L9), so a long run on Northeastern's Explorer cluster can be split across several jobs.

We haven't run this yet. Nothing on this page comes from a fine-tuned model.

The mathsthe warm-up, and why buckets set the draw rate

During warm-up the learning rate rises in a straight line from \(\eta_0 = \eta_{\text{peak}}/(W+1)\):

\[ \eta(s) = \eta_0 + (\eta_{\text{peak}} - \eta_0)\,\frac{s}{W}, \qquad s \le W. \]

With \(W = 100{,}000\) and \(\eta_{\text{peak}} = 10^{-4}\): \(\eta(50{,}000) \approx 5.0 \times 10^{-5}\) and \(\eta(2{,}000) \approx 2 \times 10^{-6}\).

A bucket with share \(q\) and \(F\) frames gives each of its frames a draw rate proportional to \(q/F\). Home data: \((0.3 / 450{,}561) \,/\, (0.6 / 187{,}135) = 0.21\).

Check yourselfWhy keep 60% organiser data, when our own data is what we want it to learn?

A few dozen of our runs would otherwise be most of what it sees, and it could lose the folds it already does well. The organiser data is the closest thing to the test setup (report §9.6).

7What is still open

Ilia won the simulation round and came second on real robots. What did he leave unsolved?

  • One pipeline instead of two. His biggest regret: the real round ran as plain copying, with none of part 2's tools, because the real robot had no reward or value (report §10.5). A real-side value would bring back advantages and best-of-N, and weight corrections better than the fixed 2.0 and 0.3. He guesses more than 90% is reachable that way. Our chapter 1 is a small first step: on 21 runs, the progress head already separates folds from failures.
  • Recovering without a person. Every trick in part 2 pushes the policy toward moves it has already seen work; none invents a new way out of a bad state. His attempts to add exploration mostly pushed plans off what the model knows (report §10.4). A person is still the only reliable source of recovery.
  • A smaller model. He thinks this task could be solved with a much smaller model; he chose a large one to try full-scale fine-tuning with reinforcement learning (report §10.2). A smaller model would answer faster and train cheaper.
  • Easy starts first. In hindsight he would have spent his correction time on clean folds from easy starts, not on hard cases (report §9.10).
  • Cheap picture tricks. Simple image changes during training (colour, blur, crop, noise) made his policy robust to a moved camera or new lighting, and he finds them underused in robotics (report §10.3).
Check yourselfWhy isn't the progress head already a value?

It says how far along a fold looks. A value says how likely this run is to end in success from here. The two agree on clean runs and part ways when a run looks nearly done but will fail, the trap of part 2, chapter 3. Ours separating 21 runs is a hint, not proof.

8How to take part

You want to run it yourself. What do you do?

  1. Come during lab hours. Yash starts the model server on the GPU computer; it stops itself after half an hour with no requests.
  2. On the rig computer, double-click Start Phi Studio and choose the folding policy.
  3. Lay the garment flat and centred, hands out of the way. Someone always watches the arms, ready to stop them.
  4. Write down every run: the garment, how it was laid, and whether it folded. Failures too (chapter 2).
  5. Once the leaders can follow again, the runs that need it become corrections (chapter 5), and those feed the fine-tune (chapter 6).

9The whole of part 3

From where we are to a better policy, in order?

  1. Run the policy as it is and write down every outcome. Today: towel 10 of 10 laid square, shorts 0 of 10. (chapter 1)
  2. Read counts as ranges: n in a row means at least n/(n+4). (2)
  3. Use the logs to rule causes out before guessing: for the shorts, not the label alone. (3)
  4. Take the free wins: park when progress holds above 0.9, set the garment in tests, ask for the next plan early. (4)
  5. Turn leader tracking back on, then record short corrections where the policy goes wrong. (5)
  6. Fine-tune from the real weights with our corrections in the 30% bucket, the warm-up set for the run. (6)
  7. Count again, with ranges. (2)

10How this page was checked

Why should you trust any of this?

Whether a run folded is what the person watching wrote down on the day. Everything else about a run is read from its own log or recording each time the page is built. Every derived number is recomputed by a script against Ilia's code and config, openpi's optimizer, the report's tables, the checkpoint on disk, or a closed form, and the build refuses to finish if any check fails. What the checking caught: we expected our recordings to include the waits between plans, so our own frames would need a speed-up of about 1.8. They don't include them; the robot program writes one frame per move. A first plan for this page said 97 of 100 runs to claim 90%; the formula says 96.
