Why WAMs?

Most robot brains look and react. A world action model looks, imagines the next second, and then moves to make that imagined second happen. We ran one in a simulated kitchen to see what that is like from the inside, and to find out whether the imagining is worth what it costs.

Press Play. What it pictured and what then happened match well, and the robot finishes the task. What it costs is time: with the released settings, on our GPU, it spent 3.4 seconds thinking for every 0.8 seconds of moving.

So is the imagining worth it? We follow one second of its thinking, see how it learned to think like that, then weigh the cost. The folds under each step hold the maths, the code and a drawing from first principles; the story reads without them.

1One second inside its head

The robot is LingBot-VA, an open model from Robbyant (Ant Group), run through LeRobot's code in LIBERO: a simulated kitchen with one arm, two cameras and 20 moves a second. The task is "put both moka pots on the stove", and it took 361 moves.

The robot works in rounds we call plans. In each plan it looks, imagines the next 16 moves as video, reads those 16 moves off the imagined video, plays them, and checks what really happened. Then it plans again. Everything it has seen and done recently sits in a memory it looks back at. Our run took 23 plans (the first covers 12 moves, each later one 16, and the task was done 13 moves into the last). We follow plan 3, which starts at move 28, through its five steps. (Timings come from a separate run of all 10 LIBERO-Long tasks: 10 of 10 solved.)

look→shrink→imagine→read the moves→move and check

It looks and reads

Each camera gives a 128 × 128 picture in 3 colours, so one moment is [2, 3, 128, 128]: 98,304 numbers, fresh every move. It is never told where its joints are; its cameras and past moves stand in for that.

The words take their own path. A frozen text encoder, UMT5-XXL, turns the sentence into 512 slots of 4096 numbers (a few words, the rest blanks), and a small two-layer net maps each slot to 3072, the model's width. Every layer reads the words through cross-attention: each picture token asks a question \(q\) and gets back a mix of the word slots, weighted by how well each one matches:

\[ \text{cross-attention}(q) = \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{q\,K_{\text{words}}^\top}{\sqrt{128}}\Big)\,V_{\text{words}} \]

(\(K\): what each slot is about. \(V\): what it says. 128: the size of one attention head.)

It shrinks what it sees

A transformer compares every piece of its input with every other piece, so twice the pieces is four times the work, and raw pixels would be millions of pieces. So first the pictures go through a squeezer: the VAE of Wan2.2, the video generator this model grew from (part 2). It stays frozen, so a squeezed scene always means the same thing.

It takes 4 moves' worth of pictures and returns one small grid, a latent frame: 8 × 16 spots (both cameras side by side), 48 numbers each. Cut into 2 × 2 patches, the grid becomes the tokens the transformer works with:

\[ \tfrac{8 \times 16}{2 \times 2} = 32 \text{ tokens}, \qquad 48 \times 2 \times 2 = 192 \text{ numbers each} \xrightarrow{\;\times W\;} 3072 \]

\(W\) is one learned 192 × 3072 matrix. Decoded back (bottom left), a latent frame keeps its layout and loses fine texture. Everything the robot imagines lives at that resolution.

Go deepershapes and code
  1. The VAE gives a mean \(\mu\) and a spread for each spot; the code keeps \(\mu\). Its rhythm is causal, "1 + 4k": the first frame of an episode becomes one latent frame on its own, then every 4 new frames become one more. While acting, frames stream in (1, then 12, then 16 per plan) and none are lost.
  2. Each of the 48 channels is shifted and scaled with the VAE's stored statistics, \(z_c = (\mu_c - m_c)/s_c\), so every channel has about zero mean and unit spread, like the static of the next step.
  3. Compression: \(4 \times 2 \times 3 \times 128 \times 128 = 393{,}216\) numbers become \(48 \times 8 \times 16 = 6{,}144\), 64 times fewer.
  4. A plan is 4 latent frames, so 128 frame tokens; with its 16 move tokens, the transformer handles 144 new tokens per plan.

It imagines the next second

It will imagine the next 4 latent frames: the next 16 moves. It can't just output them, because the future isn't one thing. The arm could pass left or right of the pot, and a network scored by squared error learns the average: an arm swinging through the pot.

Instead it starts from static, random numbers in the shape [48, 4, 8, 16], and cleans it up a little at a time. This is flow matching. In training, a real future \(x\) is mixed with static \(\varepsilon\):

\[ x_\sigma = (1 - \sigma)\,x + \sigma\,\varepsilon \]

\(\sigma\) is how much static is left: 1 is all static, 0 is the real future. The network \(v\) sees the mix and learns the direction toward static, \(\varepsilon - x\). Many futures pass through any one point, so it learns their average direction. To imagine, it walks the other way in small clean-up steps:

\[ x \;\leftarrow\; x + v(x, \sigma)\,(\sigma_{\text{next}} - \sigma) \]

Here it is in one dimension, with two possible futures:

clean-up steps

A toy, not the robot: each line is one walk from static (left) toward two possible futures at −1.5 and +1.2 (right), using the exact best direction, which can be worked out for a toy this small.

With 1 step every walk lands on the average, the blurry middle. With more, each walk starts toward the middle and then commits: as the static thins, a point's position gives away its future, and the direction turns toward it.

The robot takes 20 clean-up steps, bunched at the noisy end, where the big decisions get made: where the arm goes, where the pot ends up.

Each step runs the network twice. A generator trained to copy recordings follows its words loosely. To make it follow them harder, every step asks two questions:

  • empty instruction: which way to some likely future of this scene? (\(v_{\text{empty}}\))
  • with the instruction: which way to the moka pots on the stove? (\(v_{\text{words}}\))

Training blanked the instruction 1 time in 10, so it can answer both. The gap between the answers is what the words add, and guidance goes 5 times as far along it. Both runs go through the network together, as one batch:

\[ v = v_{\text{empty}} + 5\,(v_{\text{words}} - v_{\text{empty}}) \]

This is classifier-free guidance (CFG); with 1 instead of 5 it would just be \(v_{\text{words}}\).

Go deeperwhy it works, and the code
  1. Training minimises \(\mathbb E\,\| v(x_\sigma, \sigma) - (\varepsilon - x)\|^2\). Fix \(\sigma\) and a point: many lines pass through it, and a squared error is smallest at the mean, so the best network is \(v^*(x_\sigma, \sigma) = \mathbb E[\varepsilon - x \mid x_\sigma]\), the average direction of the lines through that point.
  2. One big step gives the blur. At \(\sigma = 1\) the point is pure static, and static says nothing about which future it came from. So the average direction just points at the average future, \(v^* = x_\sigma - \mathbb E[x]\), and one step to \(\sigma = 0\) moves the point to \(x_\sigma - v^* = \mathbb E[x]\), wherever it started. In the toy every point lands on \(0.45(-1.5) + 0.55(1.2) = -0.015\), between the two futures and on neither.
  3. Small steps don't. The average in point 1 is taken again at every point and every \(\sigma\), not once for the whole cloud. While a point is mostly static the average aims between the futures. As the static thins, the point's position gives its future away: only lines heading to that future pass near it, so the average turns toward it. The same start, \(x = 2\), in the toy:
    StepsPath of the pointLands
    12 → −0.015on the blur
    22 → 0.99 → 1.24in the 1.2 future
    42 → 1.50 → 1.31 → 1.29 → 1.39in the 1.2 future
    202 → … → 1.58in the 1.2 future
    The first step always heads for the average; after that the field bends the point into one future. Each future here has a width (±0.25), so a start on the far right lands on the right side of its future. Other starts land in the −1.5 future, 45% of them.
  4. Why the shares come out right. Think of counting cars. Through any small stretch, the flow of points is density × average velocity. Giving each point the local average velocity instead of its own line's velocity changes no flow anywhere, so the cloud moves exactly as it would along the lines: all static at \(\sigma = 1\), all real futures at \(\sigma = 0\), each in its right share. In symbols, the density obeys \(\partial_\sigma p + \nabla\cdot(p\,v^*) = 0\). So a fresh sample of static ends as one sharp future, not as the mean.
  5. The paper writes the same line with the clock reversed, \(s = 1 - \sigma\), and the direction \(x - \varepsilon\).

The steps are spaced by one warp of evenly spaced values, \(\sigma' = 5\sigma / (1 + 4\sigma)\), so 0.95 becomes 0.99 and 0.5 becomes 0.83. Each dot is one clean-up step from the code's own scheduler; the shading is where training practises. Frames spend 17 of 20 steps noisier than halfway; moves spend 34 of 50 within 10% of clean. Training draws one \(\sigma\) per latent frame, separately for frames and moves, warped the same way, so its median is the warp of 0.5: 0.833 for frames, 0.048 for moves.

    video_noise_pred[:1] - video_noise_pred[1:])

It reads off its moves

Next come the moves: 16 of them, 7 numbers each (move the hand in x, y and z, turn it three ways, open or close the gripper). Each number runs from −1 to 1, a share of the most the hand may change in one move, so the first move's Δx of 0.60 is about 3 cm forward. They come out of static too, in 50 steps bunched at the clean end, where a millimetre matters.

How does a picture of the future give moves? Without one, "what do I do next?" has many good answers. With a picture of now and one of 4 moves later, "what move gets me from this to that?" has about one. Roboticists call that inverse dynamics, and it is the move tokens' job:

  1. One extra pass writes the 4 imagined frames into memory, beside the real past.
  2. 16 move tokens start as static, each stamped with a time between two frames.
  3. For 50 steps they run through the same 30 layers. Attention lets each one read every frame in memory, and its time stamp places it between the frame before and the imagined frame after.
  4. Out come [16, 7] moves, stretched back to LIBERO's units.

In training, the frame after was real (part 2). Here it is imagined. In symbols, the robot makes two draws, with \(\hat z_{\text{next}}\) the imagined frames:

\[ \underbrace{\hat z_{\text{next}} \sim p(\,\cdot \mid \text{past frames, past moves, words})}_{\text{imagine}} \qquad \underbrace{\text{moves} \sim p(\,\cdot \mid \hat z_{\text{next}}, \text{past}, \text{words})}_{\text{read the moves}} \]

The imagined frames do not depend on the moves: they are a plan of what should happen, not a forecast of what a move would cause.

A VLA makes the same left-or-right choice inside one step, from now straight to moves. LingBot-VA splits it: pick one future as a picture, then read the nearly fixed moves off it. The hard part becomes predicting video, which can learn from video alone; the part that needs recorded moves becomes easy.

Go deeperone network, two jobs

Imagining frames and reading off moves are two jobs for the same 30 layers. When acting they take turns: each pass through the network does one job, on frame tokens or on move tokens. (In training both go through together; see part 2.)

What each job has to itself:

  • Its own doors. A frame token is 192 numbers and a move is 30. Each job has a matrix that widens its tokens to 3072, the width every layer works in, and one that narrows them back.
  • Its own dials. The noise level becomes the 6 dials of every layer (see "one clean-up step") through a separate small net for each job.

Why a move is 30 numbers when LIBERO needs 7. One layout fits every robot: per arm, a 7-number hand pose, up to 7 joint angles and the gripper, times 2 arms. LIBERO uses 7 of the 30. A plan's 16 moves are stored as [30, 4, 4]: 30 numbers, 4 latent frames, 4 moves for each.

How attention knows when a move happens. Every token is stamped with a time. Frames sit at whole numbers; the 4 moves that lead from frame 0 to frame 1 sit at 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8. RoPE turns part of each token's numbers by an angle set by its time, so attention compares two tokens by how far apart in time they are.

It moves, then checks

The arm plays the 16 moves without looking: 0.8 seconds. The cameras keep recording. Then the robot swaps: it throws away the 4 frames it imagined for this plan and writes the real ones in their place, squeezed the same way, with the 16 moves it actually made. The next plan imagines from what really happened, not from its own guess, so errors can't pile up.

The memory is the recent past the model looks back at whenever it imagines or reads moves: the real frames and moves of the last 15 plans, about 12 seconds. When it is full, the oldest plan drops out.

Why not feed it the whole past again each time? That would mean pushing all of it through the 30 layers at every pass, 74 passes per plan. Instead, the first time a token goes through, each layer keeps the two things attention will need from it later, its key and its value, and later passes reuse them. That store is the KV cache.

Go deeperwhat it costs

Size: 2 (keys and values) × 2 (the guidance pair) × 2,160 tokens × 3,072 numbers × 2 bytes × 30 layers = 1.59 GB. Per plan the model computes 144 new tokens against at most 2,160 keys, the size of the memory, instead of re-reading the whole history. Per plan it runs the network 74 times: 21 frame passes (20 clean-up steps plus the one that writes the imagined frames into memory) and 51 move passes (50 + 1), each as a pair for guidance, plus 2 passes that write reality back (72 on the first plan).

2How it learned that

The network didn't start from nothing. It started as Wan2.2, a video generator trained on web video, so it already had a rough sense of how scenes move. Robbyant then trained it on about 16,000 hours of robots with their moves recorded, then on LIBERO, 50 recordings per task. The middle stage matters: skipping it gives 80.6% instead of 92.9% on RoboTwin (easy), a two-arm benchmark.

Every stage uses one trick. Lay each recording out twice: one copy as recorded, one buried in static, a random amount per latent frame. The network learns to clean up the buried copy, frames and moves, while reading the clean copy as context.

A mask sets who may read what. Noisy frames read only the real past, so they learn to picture what comes next. Noisy moves read the real past and the real frames of their own plan, so they learn the move that produced those frames: inverse dynamics. Both are scored the same way:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \big\| v(x_\sigma) - (\varepsilon - x) \big\|^2_{\text{frames}} + \big\| v(a_\sigma) - (\varepsilon - a) \big\|^2_{\text{moves}} \]

(The code also weights each term by its noise level; the training fold draws the weights.)

Here is the gap: when acting, those frames don't exist yet, so the moves read imagined ones. As far as we can find, nobody has measured what that costs. To soften it, half the time training smudges the clean past with static.

Tap a row: what may this token read?

Computed by running the code's own mask on a small example: 4 latent frames of 1 token each, 2 moves per frame, 2 frames per plan (V = frames, A = moves, n = noisy, c = clean). The rule in one line: give the frames of plan \(k\) the number \(2k\) and its moves \(2k + 1\); clean reads clean up to its own number; noisy reads clean with a smaller number; noisy reads noisy only with the same number. Noisy moves of plan \(k\) (\(2k+1\)) can therefore read the clean frames of plan \(k\) (\(2k\)), which is the inverse-dynamics rule. The smudging of the past goes up to 83% static in the code, against at most 50% in the paper.

3Is the imagining worth it?

First, the bill. Here is where one plan's time went in our run:

Part of it is wasted: each move pass also runs an empty-instruction copy for guidance, but move guidance is 1, so that copy is thrown away.

The paper's own settings are leaner: 3 clean-up steps for frames, stopping before the picture is finished, and 10 for moves, a hint that the moves need the layout, not the detail. It also plans the next chunk while the arm moves. Neither is in the release we ran.

Planning while moving is harder than it looks. The newest real picture is already old, and planning from the model's own imagined outcome brings back the drift that writing reality into memory avoids: in the paper, three-stage tasks fall from 93.2% to 32.9%. The fix is a forward model: imagine what the running moves will produce, then plan from that. Unlike the plan, this is a true "what if I do this" forecast.

Could you keep what video teaches and skip imagining when acting? Fast-WAM (March 2026) tested that with four versions of one model on the same data: no video; Fast-WAM, video in training only; imagine then act, one model imagines and another reads the moves; joint denoising, one network cleans frames and moves together. LingBot-VA sits between the last two.

Removing video from training costs 8 points on RoboTwin. Keeping it in training but skipping imagining when acting moves success by about a point either way, at a quarter to a third of the delay. If that holds, video's value is in what it teaches, and the pictures at run time are optional. Two cautions: one group built all four versions, and one independent study found the imagining models weaker when the camera is moved (45.6% for π0.5, which has no video, against 28.9% for LingBot-VA).

Companies split the same way. Dyna, the only one with a named paying customer (its robots fold napkins at Din Tai Fung), does not imagine when acting. NVIDIA, Dream Labs and Robbyant do, with no named deployments yet. Most of these numbers are self-reported.

Imagining is getting cheaper, though. Robbyant's second version reports 142 ms per plan, down from 927 ms, with 2 clean-up steps, 8-bit arithmetic and leaner memory, plus a squeezer trained to keep meaning (78.0% to 86.6% on RoboTwin for a small test model). If those hold, the trade changes.

Our take: learning to predict video is worth it; imagining while acting has not yet earned its cost. For our two-arm SO-101 the cheap experiment is to train with video and act without it. And the most useful thing we could do for everyone is measure the bet from part 2: how much worse the moves get when they read imagined frames instead of real ones.

4Paper vs release

Paper vs releasefive differences
  • The release has one shared stack of layers, where the paper describes two networks side by side; 20 frame and 50 move clean-up steps, not 3 and 10; no plan-while-moving mode; smudging of the past up to 83% static, not 50%; and the move encoder and decoder are single matrices, not small networks.
  • The paper's text against its own tables: "10.3% better with 10 demonstrations" is the 5-demonstration gap (10 gives 7.5); "best on all six real tasks" fails for folding clothes (48.8 vs 62.9); "92.0% on RoboTwin" matches neither 92.9 nor 91.6.
  • Possible bugs in LeRobot's training path (read in the code, not run): moves enter training un-normalised; the instruction is never dropped, so the empty-prompt branch that guidance needs is never trained by fine-tuning; in training, 3 of every 16 frames are dropped by the squeezer's 1 + 4k rhythm.
