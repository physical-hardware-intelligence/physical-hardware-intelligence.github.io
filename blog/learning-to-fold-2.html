Learning to Fold, part 2: folding more often

The copying robot of part 1 folds some of the time. This part is how Ilia Larchenko's policy learned to fold four times in five in simulation: by scoring its own tries, judging each moment, and practising what worked. The numbers are from our own runs of his policy.

1Copying has a ceiling

The copying robot of part 1 folds some of the time. Why not every time?

A copier is only as good as what it copies, and it only learns what the recordings show. In the simulation round of the LeHome Challenge the starting recordings came from a scripted policy: clean folds, no mistakes, and so no recoveries (report §1.1, §2.2). A policy that copies them folds some of the time, and when it slips it has never seen what to do next.

To do better, Ilia let the policy practise. A simulator can fold thousands of times with nobody watching. His pool at the end held about 12,500 practice episodes, 4.3 million frames, and he generated a few times more over the run (report §8.2). The question this part answers: how does a policy learn from its own tries, when nobody tells it which move was right? That is reinforcement learning, in a gentle form.

We ran his simulation policy on 16 garments it never trained on: 13 folded on the first run, and all 16 when we ran the same 16 starts again. Only 6 of the 16 came out exactly the same, outcome and length. The starting noise from part 1's chapter 3 makes every run different, and that difference is what practice learns from.

The loop in four words: try, score, judge, practise. Chapters 2 to 6 take them one at a time.

The mathswhat practice is trying to raise

Reinforcement learning looks for the policy with the highest expected return, the total reward of an episode:

\[ J(\pi) = \mathbb E_{\pi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r_t\Big]. \]

Ilia builds the reward so that every episode's total is exactly its outcome, \(\sum_t r_t = \mathbb 1[\text{success}]\) (chapter 2), so \(J(\pi)\) is simply the policy's success rate. Everything on this page is a way to raise that number without a person labelling which moves were good.

Check yourselfWhy can't copying clean recordings teach recovery?

Recordings with no mistakes contain no moments after a mistake, so the copier never sees what a good move looks like from there (report §2.2, §10.4).

2Scoring half a fold

The simulator only says pass or fail at the very end. How do you give credit along the way?

The simulator decides success by measuring distances between marked points on the garment. Points that should meet, like the two sleeves, must come closer than a threshold; points that should stay apart must not (report §1.1). With only that final verdict, a good grasp in the first second earns nothing until the very end.

Ilia reuses the same distance tests to add a checkpoint partway: reaching it is worth 0.5, and success brings the total to 1.0. For tops the first half arrives gradually, in step with how far the main distance has closed (report §6.1). Here is a fold from our repeat run:

One more rule: if an episode fails, everything it earned is taken back, spread over the frames after its best moment. So a whole episode is always worth exactly 1 or 0 (report §6.1). The checkpoints give credit inside an episode; the total stays honest about the outcome.

The mathsthe gradual checkpoint and the withdrawal

For tops, let \(d_t\) be the main distance as a fraction of its threshold, \(m_t = \min_{\tau\le t} d_\tau\) its lowest value so far, and \(t_1\) the frame the first checkpoint is reached. The running total from that checkpoint is

\[ R^{\text{cp1}}_t = 0.5\,\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{d_0 - m_t}{d_0 - d_{t_1}},\,0,\,1\right), \qquad r_t \mathrel{+}= R^{\text{cp1}}_t - R^{\text{cp1}}_{t-1}. \]

If the episode fails, with \(t_p\) the last frame where a reward brought the total to its peak, the total is taken back evenly over the remaining frames:

\[ r_t \mathrel{-}= \frac{\sum_\tau r_\tau}{T - t_p - 1}, \qquad t_p < t < T, \qquad \text{so} \quad \sum_t r_t = \mathbb 1[\text{success}]. \]

Check yourselfWhy take the reward back on failure, instead of keeping the checkpoint credit?

A checkpoint can hold while the rest of the garment is in a state that makes success unlikely. Keeping its credit would reward progress that misled (report §6.1).

3The policy judges itself

To know which moments were good, you need to know how each one was going. Who decides that?

Reinforcement learning usually trains a second network for this job, a critic. Ilia puts it inside the policy. Remember the query from part 1, the blank token that sees only the pictures? It feeds a small head that predicts the chance this episode ends in success (report §5.1). Because a whole episode is worth exactly 1 for success, that chance, minus the reward already collected, is the reward still to come: the value (report §6.2).

Here is the same short-sleeved top, from the same starting layout, run twice on our GPU:

The forecast has weak spots. On easy garments it sits above 0.9 and says little, and it drifts, because the same network is also learning to move (report §6.3). So a second head predicts how far along the fold is, from 0 to 1, trained only on episodes that succeeded. In the failed run above it ended at : from the pictures the fold looked nearly done when it wasn't, a trap the report names (§6.2).

The real checkpoint keeps that progress head and retrained it on real recordings (report §9.5). On our table it still tells a fold from a failure:

The mathssuccess chance as a value, smoothed

With \(\sum_t r_t = \mathbb 1[\text{success}]\) and \(R^{\text{cum}}_t\) the reward collected before \(t\), the expected reward still to come is

\[ V(s_t) = \mathbb E\Big[\sum_{\tau \ge t} r_\tau \,\Big|\, s_t\Big] = P(\text{success}\mid s_t) - R^{\text{cum}}_t . \]

The head's raw guesses are noisy, so every use takes an exponential moving average, \(\bar S_t = 0.2\,\hat P_t + 0.8\,\bar S_{t-1}\). Near a successful ending the head is biased low, because "almost done" frames mostly come from failed episodes, which sit in that state for a long time. Offline, where the outcome \(y\) is known, the last 30 frames of \(\bar S\) are replaced by a straight line to \(y\) (report §6.2).

The progress head is trained on \(t/T\) in successful episodes only, with a squared error.

Check yourselfWhy train the progress head only on episodes that succeeded?

In a failed episode, \(t/T\) doesn't measure how much of the fold is done: the fold never got done. Only in a success does the last frame mean "finished".

4Better than expected

An episode succeeded. Was every move in it a good one?

No. Some moves made the fold more likely, some wasted time, and some made things worse before luck rescued them. What matters for one move is whether things went better than expected after it. That is its advantage. The simplest version: after each move, how much did the success forecast rise?

Ilia mixes in the checkpoint reward too, and trusts the forecast only halfway, because it is a model's guess. With \(\alpha = 0.5\), each step scores roughly \((1-\alpha)\) times the reward plus \(\alpha\) times the rise in the forecast (report §6.2). Trust the forecast fully and the reward all but cancels out; ignore it and only the checkpoints speak. Progress adds a small push of its own (report §6.4). Then each move's score adds up what followed it, fading by 0.989 per step, so a grasp gets credit for the fold it set up.

Real data: the forecast, progress and reward of the folded short-sleeved top above, computed with a port of Ilia's code that matches his on every one of the 4,138 frames of our run.

This is the part of the advantage one episode decides. Ilia then blends in an outcome-only score for older episodes, normalises across the whole pool and clips; those steps need all 12,500 episodes, so they are left out here (report §6.5, §6.6).

The mathswhy trust the forecast only partly, and the backward sum

Let \(G_t = \mathbb 1[\text{success}] - R^{\text{cum}}_t\) be the reward that actually came after \(t\), and \(\hat V_t\) the model's estimate of it. Subtracting a fraction of the estimate, \(\hat A_t = G_t - \alpha \hat V_t\), has variance

\[ \operatorname{Var}(G - \alpha \hat V) = \operatorname{Var}(G) - 2\alpha\operatorname{Cov}(G,\hat V) + \alpha^2 \operatorname{Var}(\hat V), \qquad \alpha^\star = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(G,\hat V)}{\operatorname{Var}(\hat V)} . \]

If the estimate is the truth plus independent noise, \(\alpha^\star = \sigma_V^2/(\sigma_V^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) = \rho^2 < 1\): the worse the forecast, the less of it to subtract (report §6.2). Ilia used \(\alpha = 0.5\); estimated afterwards on his rollouts, \(\alpha^\star\) ranged from about 0.4 to 0.8.

With \(\hat V_t = \alpha(\bar S_t - R^{\text{cum}}_t)\), one step of the sum is

\[ \delta_t = r_t + \gamma \hat V_{t+1} - \hat V_t = (1 - \alpha\gamma)\,r_t + \alpha\,(\gamma \bar S_{t+1} - \bar S_t) + \alpha(1-\gamma)\,R^{\text{cum}}_t , \]

and the advantage adds the future back in, \(A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda A_{t+1}\), with \(\gamma = 0.999\), \(\lambda = 0.99\) (GAE). Progress adds \(\alpha_c(\gamma \bar C_{t+1} - \bar C_t)\) through the same backward sum (report §6.4).

Check yourselfWith α = 1, what does the checkpoint reward add to a move's score?

Almost nothing. It is already counted in the reward collected so far, so it cancels against the value, and only the change in the success forecast is left (report §6.2). With \(\gamma = 0.999\) the cancelling is not quite exact: the reward keeps \(1 - \alpha\gamma = 0.001\) of its size. Try it on the slider.

5Practise what worked

Every frame now has a score. How does the policy learn from it?

The best-known reinforcement learning methods, PPO and its relatives, push the probability of good actions up and of bad actions down. That needs each action's probability, which a flow-matching policy doesn't give cheaply, and pushing away from bad actions tends to push its plans off the thin set of motions that actually work (report §2.2).

So Ilia only ever pushes toward good actions the policy already makes: it practises the good moments more often (advantage-weighted regression, Peng et al., 2019). Each frame is drawn for training with a probability that grows like \(e^{A}\), with \(A\) clipped to between −2 and 2, so a frame at the top is drawn about 55 times as often as one at the bottom. The training step itself is still part 1's plain copying (report §2.3).

A toy, computed exactly: 70% of the moves are poor ones near −1, 30% good ones near +1, and a move's advantage is its position. Not our data.

The success and progress heads must not be fooled by this tilt: they predict over the real mix of moments, not the tilted one. So every drawn frame carries a weight that undoes its extra draws, for the heads only (report §2.3).

The mathswhere e^A comes from, and why sampling is the same

AWR asks for the policy that gains the most advantage while staying close to the data's policy \(\mu\):

\[ \max_\pi\ \mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[A(s,a)] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \mu) \le \epsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi^\star(a\mid s) \propto \mu(a\mid s)\, e^{A(s,a)/\beta} \]

(Peng et al., 2019). Fitting \(\pi^\star\) by copying the data with weights \(e^{A/\beta}\) is the same, in expectation, as copying the data drawn with probability proportional to those weights. Ilia draws: \(P(i) \propto e^{\mathrm{clip}(A_i,-2,2)}\), \(\beta = 1\). A frame at weight \(e^{-2}\) is almost never loaded, so no batch slot is spent on it.

The heads get importance weights \(w_i = 1/(N\,p_i\,T_{\mathrm{ep}(i)})\), so their averages are over the data, not the tilt.

Check yourselfWhy draw good frames more often, instead of weighting their loss?

In expectation it's the same. But a frame with weight \(e^{-2}\) almost never gets loaded, so its pictures are never decoded and it takes no room in a batch: all the compute goes to the moves worth learning (report §2.3).

6Ask for the good version

Could the policy learn what a good move looks like, and then be asked for one?

Yes: give it one more input. In training an advantage token is switched on for frames that went better than expected and off for the rest. For good frames it is also switched off at random, between 10% and 50% of the time, so the model still learns the plain version (report §4.3). This is RECAP, from Physical Intelligence's π*0.6 (2025). When the policy runs, the token is always on: do what your good frames did.

Guidance then pushes further (classifier-free guidance, Ho and Salimans, 2022). At every nudge of part 1's chapter 3, the action expert runs twice, with the token and without, and the plan moves in the "with" direction plus \(s\) times the difference. Ilia's tuning pushed \(s\) up to between 7 and 9 (report §7.3, §7.7).

The same toy. The token is on for a move with probability Φ(A), as if each frame's advantage were measured with noise; guidance is shown in its ideal form, \(p_s \propto p_{\text{off}}^{\,1-s}\,p_{\text{on}}^{\,s}\).

On our robot none of this runs. The real checkpoint has no advantage token, so the guidance setting that the robot program sends is ignored (part 1; code L1642–1646).

The mathsthe token's schedule, and what guidance does

The token. For \(A < 0\) it is always off. For \(A \ge 0\) it is off with probability falling in a straight line from 0.5 at \(A = 0\) to 0.1 at \(A \ge 2\) (report §4.3). The same switch drives an extra vector in every layer of the action expert (part 1, chapter 6).

Guidance. With \(v_{\text{on}}\) and \(v_{\text{off}}\) the expert's two answers at one nudge,

\[ \hat v = v_{\text{off}} + s\,(v_{\text{on}} - v_{\text{off}}) . \]

For exact score functions this samples \(p_s \propto p_{\text{off}}^{\,1-s}\,p_{\text{on}}^{\,s}\): at \(s = 1\) the plain "with token" model, above 1 a sharper version that leans further toward what makes the token likely. For a learned flow and ten steps it is an approximation.

Check yourselfWhat does guidance with s = 1 do? And s = 0?

At \(s = 1\) it is just the "with token" answer. At \(s = 0\) it is the plain model, as if the token weren't there. Only \(s > 1\) extrapolates past what the token alone asks for.

7Try several, keep the best

The same weights fold more or less often depending on how they're run. Can that be tuned too?

Best-of-N. From the same snapshot, draw two or three plans, each from its own noise. Score each with a head that predicts how much that plan will raise the chance of success, and play the best. If every candidate scores below zero, draw twice as many more and pick from all of them (report §7.4). The surprise: in Ilia's tests that score barely tracked real outcomes, yet two or three candidates still beat one. His guess is that the candidates usually agree, and at the rare forks picking one avoids the worst.

Our own simulator log fits that picture: of 1,241 plans, redrew because every candidate looked bad, and even then the chosen plan scored above zero only of the time.

Seven settings, tuned while practising. How many moves to play per plan, how fast to play them, how much of the old plan to keep, when the anchor lets go, the guidance scale, the noise size and the number of candidates: each was tuned per garment during practice, not by a grid search (report §7.7). Each candidate value is an arm of a bandit with a belief about how well it does, and each practice episode tries the arms its beliefs currently favour (Thompson sampling). After the episode, its result minus that garment's average success updates the arms it used.

The mathsThompson sampling, one arm at a time

Each value \(k\) of a setting is an arm with a Beta belief \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_k, \beta_k)\) about how often it beats the garment's average. For an exploration episode, draw \(\theta_k \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_k,\beta_k)\) for every arm and use the arm with the largest draw. After the episode, with \(r = \mathbb 1[\text{success}] - \overline{\mathrm{SR}}(\text{garment})\),

\[ \alpha_k \mathrel{+}= \max(r, 0), \qquad \beta_k \mathrel{+}= \max(-r, 0). \]

The settings are tuned independently, the beliefs fade toward even each iteration so they follow the improving policy, and only unbiased episodes update them (report §7.7).

Check yourselfWhy don't replays of saved states update the bandit?

Replays start from states chosen to be easier or harder than a fresh episode, so their success rates would mislead the beliefs. They still feed training (report §3.2, §7.7).

9From the simulator to our table

It folds four times in five in simulation. Why not run that on a real robot?

It didn't work. The simulation policy had learned the simulator's quirks: it could tell apart two ways of shrinking the same picture that look identical to a person (report §9.1; part 1, chapter 7). The real recordings also folded some garments differently: the short top from the other sleeve, long pants from the other end, and the shorts turned around in half the episodes.

So in one week for the real round, Ilia started from a strong but deliberately not the latest simulation checkpoint (report §9.4). He removed everything that needs the simulator: the checker's rewards, the advantage token and guidance, best-of-N and the extra heads. And he went back to copying: 500 folds by the organisers, 792 of his own episodes at home including corrections, and 1,723 replays of successful simulated folds, mixed 60, 30 and 10 percent (report §9.5, §9.6).

That is the checkpoint of part 1. The reinforcement learning on this page shaped where it started; on our table it runs as a copier, with two of these heads retrained on real recordings: progress (chapter 3) and the garment guess (part 1).

In that mix his corrections at home were "the only source of recovery" (report §9.6), and recovering without a person is the problem he leaves open (report §10.4). Part 3 starts there.

Check yourselfWhich tools from this page still work on our robot?

The progress and garment heads, which the real checkpoint retrained. Rewards, advantages, the token, guidance and best-of-N all need either the simulator's checker or advantage labels, which the real round doesn't have (report §9.5).

10The whole of part 2

One round of practice, end to end?

  1. Rollout machines fold with the newest weights, trying several plans per snapshot and the settings the bandit favours. Each frame keeps the policy's own success and progress forecasts. (chapters 3, 7)
  2. The checker's distances turn each episode into rewards: credit at the checkpoint, everything back if it failed. (2)
  3. Before each round of training, every frame of every episode gets an advantage: reward and the rise in the forecast, mixed, plus progress, summed backwards. (4)
  4. Training draws good frames more often and copies them, with the advantage token on for the good ones. The heads learn from the untilted mix. (5, 6)
  5. New weights go back to the rollout machines, and the loop goes round again. (8)
